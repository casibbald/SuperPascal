# Offline-First Computing Education with the Zeal Computer and SuperPascal

In this report we analyze the design of an offline-first educational computing ecosystem centered on the "Zeal Computer" (an 8-bit Z80-based device with FPGA graphics/sound) and a two-tier SuperPascal language/compiler. We consider historical precedents, pedagogical principles, technical feasibility, cultural context, curriculum design, system design, and policy implications. The goal is to advise educational authorities, curriculum designers, language engineers, and implementers on creating a sustainable 10-20 year pipeline for computing education that starts with a simple 8-bit environment for beginners and transitions to a full-featured development platform for advanced students, all without relying on Internet access. The two-tier compiler model under discussion consists of:

- Tier 1 (ages  $\sim$ 10-15): a Z80-native microcompiler for a safe, simplified subset of Pascal, running directly on the Zeal Computer. This provides a constrained, easy environment for younger students.  
- Tier 2 (ages  $16+$  up to university level): a cross-compiler (written in Rust) that runs on a more powerful host (e.g. PC) and targets the Zeal's Z80 CPU. This supports the full SuperPascal language with advanced features (units/modules, classes & inheritance, virtual methods, exceptions, etc.), along with optimizations, linking, and low-level control for older students.

We will explore each dimension of this project in detail, providing historical comparisons and research-based rationale, and end with recommendations, potential pitfalls, and a roadmap for implementation and adoption.

# 1. Historical Precedents for Two-Tier Educational Compilers

Learning from 8-bit pioneers: The concept of tailoring computing tools to different skill levels has roots in the early days of microcomputers. In the late 1970s and 1980s, educational and home computers like the BBC Micro, Commodore 64, Apple II, TI-99/4A, and Amiga introduced an entire generation to programming. These systems balanced simplicity and power by often providing a simple built-in language for beginners (usually BASIC), while still allowing more advanced programming via assembly or higher-level languages for experienced users. Notably, the BBC Micro – created as part of the BBC's Computer Literacy Project – was intentionally designed to be beginner-friendly (booting straight into a BASIC prompt) yet expandable. Its success “was its simplicity and being true to its basic tenet: ‘to help a generation learn to use and code computers’” 1 . With the BBC Micro,  $80\%$  of British schools had at least one by the mid-1980s 2 , and students could start coding “HELLO WORLD” immediately at the command line in its built-in BASIC 3 . This instant access and low barrier to entry were key to not overwhelming beginners. At the same time, the BBC Micro's design left room for growth: it featured an assembly language monitor and allowed adding ROM cartridges (including Pascal or Logo) for more advanced learning 3 4 . In essence, one could begin with simple BASIC programs and later progress to assembly routines or structured languages on the same machine – an early form of the two-tier concept.

BASIC vs. structured languages: Many 8-bit micros of that era started novices on BASIC because of its gentle learning curve, but BASIC's limited structure and use of line numbers and GOTO often led to poor coding habits. In fact, computer science educators like Edsger Dijkstra famously argued that teaching unstructured BASIC to beginners could "mutilate" their ability to learn good programming practices 5 . While Dijkstra's phrasing was hyperbolic (he quipped that teaching BASIC should be a "criminal offence") 6 ), the core idea is widely accepted: a language that encourages spaghetti code can hinder students from grasping structured programming. This historical lesson motivates SuperPascal's choice: starting students in a structured Pascal subset rather than an archaic BASIC. Pascal was actually a popular teaching language by the 1980s and 1990s 7 , precisely because Niklaus Wirth designed it for clarity and structured programming. Several micros offered Pascal implementations (often as add-ons) for advanced students or college use – for example, Apple II had UCSD Pascal, and the TI-99/4A could run UCSD P-system Pascal (though it infamously required an expensive expansion box, often costing more than the TI computer itself) 8 . The presence of Pascal on those platforms was an acknowledgment that, beyond the introductory phase with BASIC, students should transition to a more structured, powerful language to learn modern programming concepts.

One machine, multiple levels of learning: The two-tier model we propose has implicit historical precedent in how these early systems were used. A beginner in 1983 might play with simple BASIC on a Commodore 64, but as they matured, they might learn to PEEK/POKE memory or even hand-code assembly to make games - essentially moving from a "safe subset" to the "full power" of the machine's capabilities. Similarly, the BBC Micro was used in schools at multiple levels - younger kids wrote BASIC loops, older or more advanced students could tackle projects in assembly or structured languages, all on the same hardware. The key was that the platform (8-bit, limited environment) was consistent and always offline, so the learning curve was managed through the software and language rather than introducing entirely new hardware. This ensured continuity: students felt at home with the machine as they explored more sophisticated programming techniques, rather than being thrown into a completely new environment.

The Turbo Pascal example: Perhaps the clearest inspiration for SuperPascal's two-tier compiler approach is Turbo Pascal in the early microcomputer era. Turbo Pascal (created by Anders Hejlsberg in 1983) was a full Pascal compiler and IDE that ran on minimal hardware (CP/M and early DOS machines with as little as 64KB RAM). It demonstrated that a fast, one-pass compiler could deliver a powerful language on 8-bit systems without needing large resources. As one retrospective noted, "Pascal had a remarkable advantage... a single-pass compiler that generated machine code as it parsed the source. No intermediate representations or syntax trees - just direct translation... That feature made Pascal compilers incredibly fast." This meant a tight edit-compile-run loop even on slow machines: "Turbo Pascal's development cycle was about 5 seconds, while Turbo C's was  $\sim 40$  seconds" on comparable 1980s PCs . The quick turnaround kept learners engaged. Turbo Pascal achieved this by limiting the language to what could be compiled in one pass and by integrating the editor, compiler, and runtime into one program . Our proposed Z80 micro-compiler follows the same philosophy: a simplified Pascal that is compileable in one pass, yielding immediate feedback for the student. Historical Turbo Pascal was also essentially a two-tier system across age/experience: many high school and college students in the late 80s first encountered structured programming via Turbo Pascal (after maybe dabbling in BASIC), while professional developers also used Turbo Pascal for serious software. It scaled from education to real applications because it balanced simplicity with power. We aim for SuperPascal to achieve a similar scalable balance, but within a single coordinated ecosystem: the "micro" level for initial learning and the "full" level for advanced projects, both sharing the Pascal family syntax.

Other historical insights: Another historical precedent is the UCSD P-System (p-Code) which made Pascal portable across small machines. It traded performance for broad usability: an idealized virtual machine (p-code) ran on 8-bit and 16-bit systems, so schools could teach the same Pascal language on Apple IIs, IBM PCs, even TI minis. The lesson here is the benefit of a consistent language across hardware capabilities. We emulate that by keeping SuperPascal semantics consistent between the Z80 and the cross-compiled environment – the underlying implementation differs (native vs. cross), but from the student's perspective it's the "same language". Early computing education projects also integrated curriculum with hardware/software. The BBC Micro came with not just a machine but TV programs and books teaching programming 12 13. Similarly, our initiative should produce not just compilers, but also learning materials and an integrated experience for students (more on that in later sections). The common thread from historical systems is: start simple, provide room to grow, and keep the learning environment consistent and controlled. That is exactly what the Zeal Computer with SuperPascal's two tiers attempts to do – in a modernized way appropriate for today's educational needs.

# 2. Pedagogical Rationale: Simplicity, Scaffolding, and Age-Appropriate Complexity

Why avoid complexity for beginners: Foundational programming courses for children deliberately minimize complexity to avoid cognitive overload. Young learners (especially pre-teens) have limited working memory and are still developing abstract reasoning abilities. A programming language or environment that is too feature-rich or syntactically unforgiving can overwhelm them, leading to frustration rather than learning. Educational research supports using simplified or block-based languages for beginners: "Block-based programming takes away much of the frustration for young coders (e.g. missing a comma or parenthesis) and leaves more time to focus on core concepts." 14 . Tools like MIT Scratch use drag-and-drop blocks to sidestep syntax errors and allow creativity, precisely so that children aren't discouraged by incidental complexity. While SuperPascal is a text-based language (more advanced than Scratch's visual blocks), our Tier-1 subset is designed to be as simple and error-proof as possible within a textual paradigm: e.g. no convoluted syntax, a small number of keywords/constructs, and perhaps constraints that prevent confusing language features. The goal is to make the first contact with coding positive and manageable – students can write a program with just a few lines and see it work, reinforcing their confidence.

Educational theory such as cognitive load theory and scaffolding emphasizes that newcomers learn best when material is introduced in digestible pieces, building on prior knowledge gradually  $^{15}$ $^{16}$ . Our approach is one of scaffolding: the micro-compiler exposes only a safe subset of Pascal, which limits the scope to fundamentals (variables, basic types, loops, conditionals, simple I/O, and perhaps simple procedures). By constraining what beginners can do, we actually reduce extraneous cognitive load and guide them to focus on core concepts like algorithmic thinking and structured problem-solving  $^{15}$ . There is evidence that such scaffolding improves learning outcomes in programming by allowing students to master basics before moving on  $^{17}$ . For example, ScratchJr, designed for ages 5-7, famously omits even variables and loops because those concepts may be too abstract for that age; it sticks to very simple sequential commands suitable for pre-readers  $^{18}$ . As age increases, more features can be introduced. In our pipeline, by age  $\sim 10$  a child is usually reading and doing basic math, so a simple text language is feasible, but we still intentionally avoid advanced concepts (no pointers, no objects, no concurrency, etc. in the subset). These features are deferred to later (Tier-2) when the student's cognitive readiness is higher.

Age and absorption of advanced concepts: Research in developmental psychology (notably Piaget's stages) indicates that around 11-12 years old children enter the formal operational stage where they become capable of more abstract and systematic thinking  $^{19}$ $^{20}$ . Prior to roughly age 11, most children are in the concrete operational stage - they learn better with concrete examples and struggle with abstraction or hypothetical reasoning  $^{20}$ . This has direct implications for teaching programming: complex abstractions like object-oriented design, pointers/reference semantics, or high-level type theory may not be meaningful or necessary for younger students. A 10-year-old can certainly grasp logic and sequencing, and even somewhat abstract ideas like variables (as long as they're related to something concrete, like a "score" in a game). But something like class inheritance or recursion might be a leap too far until they develop more abstract thinking ability in their teens. Cognitive science supports this progression: "Abstract thought emerges during the formal operational stage (early adolescence,  $\sim$ 11-15); children think very concretely in earlier stages, and only then begin to consider hypothetical outcomes and abstract concepts."  $^{21}$ $^{22}$ . Therefore, we plan when to introduce certain CS concepts based on age. For example:

- Ages 10-12: Focus on concrete programming tasks – simple calculations, drawing shapes on screen, basic game logic. Keep concepts tangible (loop as “repeat these steps X times”, variable as a “box” holding a number). No need for extensive theory of types or any form of indirection. Error handling can be minimal (the micro-compiler can simply stop on an error rather than students dealing with exceptions).  
- Ages 13-15: At this stage early formal thinking kicks in. We can introduce slightly more abstract ideas like procedures with parameters (problem decomposition), perhaps the concept of a function returning a value, or simple user-defined data types (like enumerations or records) to model things. The language subset can expand a bit here (for instance, allow defining simple records or using library routines that involve light concepts of data structures). Still, features like inheritance or polymorphism are probably not yet appropriate – those remain Tier-2 topics. We might introduce the idea of an object in a very concrete way (e.g. a sprite in a game that has properties and behaviors) without the full syntax of class definitions.  
- Ages 16-18: Now students are typically capable of reasoning abstractly and handling multiple concepts at once. This is the right time to introduce object-oriented programming, dynamic data structures (linked lists, trees), more complex algorithms, and also the underlying system concepts. Tier-2 SuperPascal enables these: classes, inheritance, virtual methods (introducing polymorphism), as well as pointers (with appropriate safety). We also introduce error/exception handling in code, and perhaps generics (depending on how Rust-like we make the advanced Pascal). By late high school, many students worldwide learn Java or C++; introducing OOP in Pascal at this stage is in line with that, just using our controlled environment.  
- University level: The full Pascal (Tier-2) can be used to teach systems programming concepts, compiler construction, operating systems, etc., because it has the needed features (and the platform can be used to demonstrate low-level behavior). At this level, students can even engage with why certain features were omitted in Tier-1 and discuss design trade-offs – turning the two-tier model itself into a learning case about language and compiler design.

Educational research on progression: The notion of gradually increasing complexity is backed by curricula guidelines. The U.S. K-12 Computer Science Framework and CSTA standards, for example, expect students by middle school (approx. age 11-14) to be comfortable with basic algorithms and to begin understanding how software and hardware interact, and by high school (14-18) to tackle abstraction and modularity in programs 23 24 . Specifically, by the end of 8th grade, students work with procedures and may use parameterization, but the design of full object-oriented systems is more often seen by Grades 10-12. Our plan aligns well with these standards: fundamentals first, advanced topics later.

Additionally, the concept of "low floor, high ceiling" is relevant. A good educational language provides an easy entry (low floor) but allows sophisticated projects (high ceiling) as students gain expertise. BASIC had a low floor but also a relatively low ceiling - beyond a point, students hit the limitations of unstructured spaghetti code and lack of abstraction. Logo (another 80s teaching language) had great concepts (like recursion via turtle graphics) but wasn't used for large programs. Pascal historically offered a higher ceiling while maintaining a reasonable floor. With SuperPascal, we ensure the floor for Tier-1 is even lower (by cutting out complexity) and the ceiling for Tier-2 is quite high (nearly on par with a modern programming language, constrained only by the 8-bit hardware). This encourages a "stretch-to-fit" pedagogy: the language environment grows with the student.

Avoiding early overwhelm: One key pedagogical reason to split the compiler into two tiers is to avoid overwhelming novices with the full complexity of a modern language or development environment. If a 11-year-old were placed directly in a professional IDE with full Rust or C++ at their fingertips, the result would likely be confusion or reliance on trial-and-error without understanding. By contrast, presenting a pared-down Pascal environment on the Zeal machine – with perhaps a simple text editor and a compile/run button – keeps things comprehensible. The child only has to learn a small set of rules initially. This ties to Papert's concept of “mathematical epistemology for kids” – giving children a microworld they can fully grasp and experiment in, such as Logo's turtle geometry. Here, the Zeal + microPascal is their comprehensible microworld. It has limitations (which are actually a pedagogical strength at first), and as they master it, they naturally crave more capability, at which point we introduce Tier-2. In fact, anecdotal evidence shows that after a couple of years with Scratch (visual coding), many middle-schoolers want to move to “real code” and are willing to handle more complexity to feel like they’re doing what grown-up programmers do  $^{25}$ . We anticipate a similar hunger in students who outgrow the micro-compiler: by age 15 they will be eager to use more powerful tools – and that motivation will make them appreciate learning about the more complex aspects of programming.

Cognitive and social aspects: It's worth noting that programming at a young age isn't just about the language features - it's also about developing confidence and problem-solving mindset. By intentionally avoiding overly advanced features early on, we let kids experience success and enjoyment. They can complete projects (a small game, a math quiz program, controlling a robot, etc.) with the subset language and feel a sense of accomplishment. This positive feedback loop is crucial in education. As one educator put it: "starting with text-based code also works if you have the right projects; the key is not the language but teaching how to solve problems with code". In SuperPascal's design, we plan to include a rich set of examples and libraries appropriate for each level (e.g. a simple graphics library for Tier-1 that abstracts away hardware details, so kids can draw or animate without needing to know about memory addresses or buffers). This ties into cognitive scaffolding: the environment should highlight the concept being learned and hide incidental complexity until the student is ready to understand it.

Finally, from a cognitive development standpoint, we also consider attention span and engagement. Younger students (pre-teens) often learn best by doing and seeing immediate results, and their attention can wane with long, abstract lectures. The offline Zeal computer with its instant-on, distraction-free interface and quick compile-run cycle will support a learn-by-doing approach. They can iteratively tweak code and see changes, which reinforces learning (the importance of fast feedback was learned early on with tools like Turbo Pascal, as mentioned, which turned long compile waits into seconds 10). Since there is no Internet or other apps on the device, the student's focus remains on the coding task or game at hand - a significant advantage for pedagogy (discussed more in section 4 on safety).

Summary: Foundational pedagogy demands that we "reduce complexity to manage complexity." We do so by using a language subset for younger students that avoids advanced abstractions and pitfalls, gradually introducing more complex concepts as students mature cognitively and academically. By aligning the introduction of language features with developmental readiness (e.g. wait for formal logical reasoning ability before introducing OOP or pointers), we maximize the chances that students will actually absorb and understand those concepts, rather than just memorize syntax. This staged approach, firmly grounded in educational research and child development theory, should produce a learning progression where students build a solid base and then climb to sophisticated levels of computing knowledge confidently.

# 3. Technical Feasibility and Architecture of the Two-Tier Compiler System

Why the Z80 can't host a "full-fat" modern compiler: The Z80 microprocessor, iconic as it is, comes from an era of severe resource constraints. It has a 16-bit address bus (meaning it can directly address only 64 KB of memory), and typical systems might only provide that much RAM total. Its registers are 8-bit (with a few 16-bit register pairs) and it runs at a few MHz. By contrast, modern compilers (for languages like C++, Java, or Rust) expect megabytes of memory and significantly higher compute power. The process of compiling a modern high-level language involves multiple stages: parsing complex grammars, building abstract syntax trees, performing semantic analysis, optimization passes, register allocation, code generation, linking, etc. These tasks consume a lot of memory and CPU time, even on today's machines. On an 8-bit CPU, just storing a large parse tree could exhaust RAM, and algorithms that are linear or worse in input size could take impractically long.

Historically, some compilers were made to run on Z80 and similar CPUs, but they had to be extremely lean. A Medium article reviewing CP/M-80 compilers notes: "Compilers for CP/M 2.2 are pretty amazing. The 8080 and Z80 machines they ran on had up to 64k of RAM but most had far less. To compensate for limited RAM, most of the compilers wrote temporary files to disk. But disks (floppy drives) were extremely slow... Performance was a real challenge and the dev inner loop wasn't great." [27]. In practice, multi-pass compilers on Z80 (for languages like C or Pascal) often had to swap data to disk for each pass, making compilation a minutes-long process and heavily I/O-bound. For example, a compiling job might generate intermediate code to a file in pass1, then read it in pass2, etc., just to cope with memory limits [27]. This is not the user experience we want for students - especially not younger ones who would lose patience with a multi-minute compile.

The Z80's architecture itself poses challenges for sophisticated compilation techniques. The Intel 8080 (ancester of Z80) lacked hardware support for stack-relative addressing which makes implementing local variables and recursion tricky  $^{28}$ . The Z80 improved this slightly by adding index registers (IX, IY) that can be used for indexed addressing, but using them is slow and clunky compared to modern CPUs  $^{29}$ . Turbo Pascal's original Z80 compiler actually used a clever workaround: it allocated each procedure's local variables in a fixed memory area ("workspace") and only copied that area to a stack when recursion happened  $^{31}$ . This way, most procedures could use absolute addresses for locals (fast), and recursive calls incurred an overhead only when needed by pushing the current locals to a separate recursion stack  $^{31}$ . This unconventional design highlights the lengths needed to get high-level language features (like recursion and nested procedures with local variables) working on a Z80. It worked, but introducing even more complex features (like nested function pointers, advanced type-checking across modules, etc.) would quickly hit the limits of what such tricks can achieve in 64KB. Modern compilers also generate a lot of

metadata (for example, debugging info, symbol tables, etc.); storing and managing that on an 8-bit system is near-impossible beyond trivial programs.

Therefore, trying to run a full modern compiler on the Z80 is impractical due to memory, processing throughput, and architectural limitations. It's not just theoretical - as an example, a modern Rust compiler itself takes hundreds of MBs of RAM and minutes even on a PC for non-trivial programs (Rust is an extreme case, but even something like GCC or Java compilers need tens of MB at least). The Zeal's Z80, even if clocked at a higher frequency than original (say 20 MHz), is still orders of magnitude slower than a PC and has no hardware multiply, no out-of-order execution, etc. Thus, Tier-2 must be offloaded to a host machine if we want full features and optimizations.

Feasibility of a Z80 "micro-compiler" (Tier-1): On the other hand, compiling a simplified subset of Pascal on the Z80 is quite feasible - we have historical proof in the form of Turbo Pascal 3.0 and similar systems. Turbo Pascal's CP/M version fit in 32KB of code and could compile reasonable programs entirely in memory, delivering machine code almost instantly  $^{11}$ . It achieved this by being a single-pass compiler with almost no optimization, generating code on the fly as it parsed. This is a model for our micro-compiler. Wirth's design of Pascal was explicitly amenable to one-pass compilation (e.g., functions must be declared before use, which eliminates the need for multi-pass or extensive lookahead)  $^{9}$ . A commentator noted this as a "well thought-out language syntax invented by Niklaus Wirth" that avoids the need for heavy intermediate representations  $^{9}$ . We will leverage the same property. The subset of Pascal we allow can be compiled top-down in one pass: as we read each declaration or statement, we emit Z80 machine code (or at least relocatable code) immediately, without building a large AST in memory. This keeps memory usage low. We will avoid features that complicate one-pass compilation - for instance, no arbitrary forward references (if forward function declarations are needed, we handle them minimally or not allow them in subset), no separate compilation units (everything in one source file for the micro-compiler), and limited expression complexity (so that expression parsing can be done in a recursive descent with limited backtracking).

We might also generate somewhat less optimized code in Tier-1 to keep compiler logic simple. That is acceptable because beginner programs are small and speed is not critical; simplicity and correctness are the priorities. Even with such trade-offs, the code quality should be fine for educational purposes – recall that even one-pass Pascal compilers in the 80s produced decent machine code; for example, one anecdote is that in early microcomputers, a Pascal program would compile/run faster than the equivalent C program because the C compiler's multi-pass process took so long versus Pascal's quick one-pass, despite C's generated code being a bit more optimized  $^{32}$ . For us, the micro-compiled code will run on actual Z80 hardware, but given the scale of student programs (likely a few hundred lines at most in that age group), performance is unlikely to be an issue. And if it is, that becomes a teachable moment for older students to learn about optimization in Tier-2.

One important aspect is memory. We will likely dedicate a portion of the Zeal's memory for the compiler and another for user program. Turbo Pascal famously had an IDE/compiler that also doubled as the runtime – it would compile your program into memory and then jump to it. We can do similarly: the micro-compiler can reside in ROM or high memory, read the source (perhaps from an SD card or typed in editor in RAM), produce machine code in RAM, and then execute it. Because our subset avoids dynamic linking or advanced runtime needs, the execution can simply begin at a start address after compile. We will, of course, include runtime support for basics (simple I/O, maybe graphics calls) in either ROM or as part of the compiler. This integrated environment (editor + compiler + runtime) on the Zeal ensures a streamlined user experience for the student – they hit “Run” and within a second or two their program is running, even on the 8-bit

machine. This is crucial for keeping young learners engaged and matches the simplicity principle we observed with Turbo Pascal's design 11 .

Architecture of the host-based cross-compiler: For Tier-2, the heavy lifting is done on a modern system (perhaps a PC or a Raspberry Pi that the older students have access to). We are implementing this compiler in Rust, which provides memory safety and modern tooling, making the compiler itself easier to maintain (and ironically, possibly easier to eventually have students contribute to, since Rust is becoming popular in systems programming education). The cross-compiler will target the Z80, essentially acting like a contemporary LLVM or GCC back-end but specialized for our Pascal dialect. The architecture likely involves parsing the full Pascal language (including all the advanced features we want to support at this level), producing an intermediate representation (IR) or abstract syntax tree, performing semantic analysis (type checking, etc.), then generating Z80 assembly or machine code. Because this runs on a modern host, we can afford multi-pass optimization and analysis – meaning this compiler can produce much more optimized Z80 code than the micro-compiler does. For instance, it could do global register allocation across the whole program, inline small functions, unroll loops, or use advanced calling conventions, all of which would be too complex for the micro-compiler. This means advanced students not only get more language features, but their programs can run faster or handle larger tasks on the same Z80 hardware, thanks to cross-compilation. It essentially breathes "new life" into the 8-bit CPU by leveraging modern compute to overcome 8-bit limitations.

One design goal is that both compilers generate compatible outputs. Likely, we will define a standard object format or ABI for the Zeal Computer (e.g., something like an object file with Z80 machine code and symbols). The cross-compiler will definitely need to support separate compilation (units/modules, linking) for large projects. The micro-compiler might not do linking (perhaps it only handles one source at a time, as many early Pascal environments did), but it should still adhere to the same calling conventions and memory layout as the cross-compiler. This way, code produced by one can interoperate with code from the other. For example, it should be possible (in principle) for an advanced student to write a library in the cross-compiler (with perhaps some complex algorithm), and a younger student to call that library from a micro-compiled program (the library being provided as a compiled binary). Or vice versa: a student could take a simple program they wrote on the micro-compiler and later compile it with the cross-compiler - it should run identically (just faster, or allowing a larger input size, etc.). We want to avoid a schism where Tier-1 programs and Tier-2 programs live in separate universes. They're all SuperPascal programs targeting the Zeal, just compiled with different toolchains. Ensuring a consistent ABI (application binary interface) - e.g., how functions are called, how parameters are passed (registers vs stack), how the stack frame is managed - is important. We will likely document a simple ABI (perhaps similar to how Turbo Pascal or C compilers for Z80 did it) and implement it in both compilers.

Interoperability without fragmentation: A potential risk of having two compilers is language divergence – where the subset evolves separately from the full language. We will mitigate this by design: the syntax and semantics of the common features are identical in both tiers. For example, if the subset allows for loops, the rules (such as the loop variable being read-only inside the loop in standard Pascal) are the same in the full compiler. If the subset has only integer and char types (for instance), the full compiler will treat those exactly the same way (just that it additionally has real, string, etc.). Essentially the subset is a true subset. This way, any program written in Micro-Pascal is also a valid program in full SuperPascal (assuming it doesn't use unsupported library calls). This compatibility is analogous to, say, how UCSD Pascal and ISO Pascal shared a large common core, or how later on languages like C had “freestanding” subsets for embedded systems. We also plan for the runtime library to be shared: e.g., the same routine that handles

writing to the screen or drawing a pixel should be accessible from both. The micro-compiler might even defer certain operations to a runtime package that is identical to what the cross-compiler links with. This not only avoids duplication of effort, but it ensures consistency. If a bug is fixed in a library routine, it benefits both tiers.

From an implementation perspective, the cross-compiler can serve as a "gold standard" for language correctness (since it's easier to update and more thoroughly test on a PC), and the micro-compiler can be a simpler implementation that might have a few intentional limitations. For instance, the micro-compiler might not implement the full general case of something if it never arises in subset usage – but it will never implement conflicting behavior. We might also embed some safeguards: if a student somehow uses an advanced construct in the micro environment (say they found an unsupported keyword), the micro-compiler will reject it with a clear message (scaffolding the learning – "this feature is available in the advanced environment"). Meanwhile, the cross-compiler could have a "subset mode" or warnings to indicate if a program stays within the subset (useful for teachers who want to ensure they aren't introducing forbidden complexity too soon). This way, no matter which compiler is used, the SuperPascal language feels like one continuous language, not two dialects. Fragmentation is a serious concern to avoid – one lesson from languages like BASIC is that having many dialects (each machine had its own BASIC with different quirks) caused confusion. By contrast, standard Pascal was... well, standard (at least once ISO Pascal was out), so code could be shared and textbooks applied broadly. We want SuperPascal to be "standard" across the two tiers.

Toolchain integration: Both compilers will produce machine code for the same target hardware (the Zeal's Z80 + FPGA system). Likely, the cross-compiler will output a binary executable or possibly an Intel HEX file or something that can be transferred to the Zeal Computer (via serial, SD card, etc.). Part of the ecosystem design is how students using the Tier-2 compiler actually run their programs on the Zeal. If they are using a PC-based IDE, they might compile on the PC and then either download the code to the Zeal via a cable or perhaps run it on an FPGA simulator or emulator on the PC first. Given the offline-first philosophy, probably in classroom the Zeal computers themselves would be network-isolated. Possibly older students will have both a PC and a Zeal device at their desk (PC for coding, Zeal for running), or the Zeal could have a mode where it acts as a device that can receive code from the PC. These logistical choices will be worked out, but technically we must ensure the format is convenient (maybe a simple tap file or snapshot that loads directly into Zeal memory).

We should also consider a debugger or at least some debugging aids. On the micro-compiler, debugging might be very primitive (perhaps outputting values to screen, etc., or using an LED if available). On the cross-compiler, we could provide a source-level debugger that runs on PC but controls the Zeal remotely (since we have full knowledge of symbol tables etc. in the compiled program). This could greatly aid teaching at the advanced level - e.g., stepping through assembly to see how high-level code executes. These tools, if provided, should again work seamlessly with the environment. It's a systems design implication that the IDE, compiler, and hardware form a cohesive toolchain for the student. Historically, this was a strength of integrated systems like Turbo Pascal (edit->compile->run all in one program) 11 and also of educational systems like the Logo turtle (where the language and the robot were one integrated learning system). We aim to replicate that cohesiveness: the Tier-1 environment is self-contained on Zeal, and the Tier-2 environment, while offloading to PC, should be as streamlined as possible (perhaps a one-click "compile and flash to device" operation). Minimizing friction in the toolchain is crucial, because any technical hassle (e.g. fiddling with command-line tools or dealing with incompatible file formats) would distract students and teachers from the learning objectives.

In summary, from a technical viewpoint: Tier-1 micro-compiler is a lean, single-pass compiler optimized for fast turnaround and low memory usage, producing straightforward code on the Z80 itself. Tier-2 crosscompiler is a full-featured optimizing compiler running on a powerful host, emitting code for Z80 and supporting all advanced language features and program sizes that the hardware can realistically handle (with the only limitations being the 8-bit machine's RAM and performance for the final execution). The two compilers will adhere to a consistent language definition and binary interface to ensure interoperability. This split architecture leverages the strength of each environment: the Z80 for its immediacy and authenticity in teaching low-level fundamentals, and the modern host for its raw power to manage complexity. We thus get the best of both worlds - keeping the on-device experience simple and responsive, while not being constrained by the device's limits when students are ready to go beyond them.

# 4. Safety, Offline Culture, and the Importance of Controlled Environments for Youth

The case for offline computing (especially in Bulgaria): In today's highly connected world, it might seem counter-intuitive to advocate disconnected computers for education. However, there is a strong movement – particularly in countries like Bulgaria – recognizing the downsides of early Internet exposure in schools. Bulgarian education authorities, responding to parent concerns, are moving to ban or limit internet-enabled devices for younger students. In fact, a new amendment (effective 2026) bans mobile phone use in all schools during class time  $^{33}$ , citing that excessive screen time and social media use outside school are harming students' focus and cognitive development: "children are becoming addicted, their concentration and critical thinking are impaired, and the function of the prefrontal cortex is altered" due to heavy device use  $^{34}$ . Bulgarian students rank among the highest in the world for daily screen time, much of it on social media rather than educational content  $^{35}$ . These stark observations have led the Ministry of Education to consider broader measures, such as ensuring that classroom computers for under-17s are not full-fledged PCs with open internet access (the user mentioned a proposed bill disallowing "full-fat OS with browser" for under-16). The rationale is clear: unrestricted internet and open computing in the classroom can introduce more harm than benefit for younger learners, including distraction, exposure to inappropriate content, cyberbullying, and plagiarism. Our project's philosophy aligns perfectly with these concerns by offering a walled-garden computing experience where students can learn programming and computing fundamentals in a safe, controlled digital space, offline.

Distraction-free learning: One of the biggest advantages of the Zeal Computer as an offline device is the lack of distractions. A typical modern computer with internet invites multitasking and off-task behavior (games, social media, etc.). In contrast, devices like the Zeal, which only run the educational software we provide, keep students on task. This was also true in the 1980s: a BBC Micro or Apple II in a classroom had nothing else to do except run the educational programs or programming environments loaded onto it. It's been noted that tech executives in Silicon Valley often choose schools (like Waldorf schools) that delay or strictly control screen time for their own kids, precisely to encourage focus and creative thinking without the dopamine hits of the internet  $^{36}$ . By removing web browsers, app stores, and the like, we essentially return to that earlier model of computing as a purposeful tool, not a general-purpose entertainment device. There's evidence that too much unfocused screen time can shorten attention spans and impede learning  $^{37}$ . A controlled device helps mitigate those issues. As the Bulgarian Education Minister quoted the Danish PM: "We have unleashed a monster. Mobile phones and social media are stealing our children's childhood."  $^{38}$ . Our solution is to tame that monster by keeping it out of the classroom until students are mature enough to handle it responsibly.

Risks of early AI and internet in programming education: The question specifically mentions AI-driven, internet-based programming environments. These include code assistants like GitHub Copilot or even using search/Stack Overflow extensively to find code solutions. While such tools are fantastic for professional productivity, they pose risks for beginners. If a student relies on an AI to write code, they bypass the very learning we want – the AI becomes a crutch, and the student may not develop problem-solving skills or a deep understanding of the code. There's already concern among educators that tools like ChatGPT can undermine learning by giving students answers or doing their homework. A recent report found  $70\%$  of teachers worry that AI use weakens students' critical thinking and problem-solving skills 39 . In programming specifically, if kids can simply prompt an AI "write a program that does X", they might get a working result, but they learn very little from the process (and may even present code they don't comprehend). Additionally, unsupervised internet access can lead to plagiarism (copy-pasting code without understanding) or exposure to toxic communities. By keeping the programming environment offline and self-contained, we force students to engage with the problems directly. They must write the logic themselves (or collaborate with peers/instructors), which builds computational thinking skills. In essence, it enforces the discipline of learning to code the way one learns to solve math problems – using one's own mind, not just looking up the answer. This disciplined approach is valued: as one educational expert said, "we shouldn't treat AI as just a cheat code; critical thinking and learning the hard way still matters" 40 .

Furthermore, AI models, as of now, can sometimes generate incorrect or subtly flawed code. A beginner cannot discern correct from incorrect code if they haven't learned the basics, so they may trust faulty AI output and get frustrated when it doesn't work, not knowing how to fix it. This could actually damage confidence. Our environment, being offline, avoids this scenario entirely by not providing such shortcuts. Of course, as students reach late high school or university  $(17+)$ , introducing AI tools with guidance could be beneficial (since part of education is learning how to leverage modern tools). But by then, in our pipeline, they will have the fundamentals and can use AI assist ethically and effectively. We essentially delay AI exposure until it's a boon, not a bane.

Cyber-safety and wellbeing: The offline approach also safeguards children from the well-documented dangers of the internet: inappropriate content (violence, pornography), online predators, cyberbullying, etc. Many parents and educators worry about these, leading to lobbying for restrictions as mentioned by the user. With Zeal Computers, even if a child tried, they cannot access a web browser or chat app – the machine simply doesn't have those. This peace of mind is important for adoption: school boards and ministries will ask, "Is it safe to give this to a 12-year-old?" We can confidently say yes, because it can't connect to external networks (except perhaps a local network for code transfer under teacher control, if even that). It's much like giving them a calculator vs. giving them a smartphone. This controlled setting allows focus on learning and also protects privacy (no data goes out, nothing from outside comes in).

Positive cultural context of an 8-bit device: Interestingly, the offline, simplified nature of the Zeal Computer might also spark a certain culture of creativity and discovery reminiscent of the 1980s home computing era. When kids aren't handed pre-made apps or infinite online entertainment, they tend to create their own fun. With a system that invites programming, students may start to tinker beyond formal lessons – writing simple games to share with friends, customizing graphics, etc. In Bulgaria and similar regions, there is already a strong tradition in informatics (competitive programming, math, etc.). An ecosystem like this could blend with that culture, giving students a tangible, hackable machine that they feel they "own" in terms of understanding. Contrast this with a locked-down iPad: an iPad is powerful but opaque – you swipe and consume. The Zeal is modest but transparent – you code and make. This cultivates a maker mindset and demystifies technology, which is a cultural goal for improving technological literacy.

Avoiding early specialization or bias: Another safety aspect is pedagogical "safety" - i.e., ensuring students don't form bad habits or misconceptions early. By controlling the environment, we can enforce good practices. For example, SuperPascal can be designed to prevent common pitfalls (it's strongly typed, catches errors). We can also avoid things like distracting fancy libraries that might encourage students to skip learning fundamentals (e.g., a beginner with Python might use a machine learning library without actually understanding the algorithm inside). In our controlled environment, if you want a result, you likely have to implement the algorithm (at least at Tier-1). This builds a strong foundation before students move on to more open environments where shortcuts exist.

Addressing concerns of parents and educators: Parents who lobbied against internet access before a certain age will be pleased to see an alternative that still teaches computing without the internet. We essentially answer the question: "How can we teach coding and CS to kids without giving them full computers that might distract or expose them to harm?" The answer is this specialized device and language. For the Ministry of Education, there's also appeal in terms of control: these devices can be standardized, monitored, and easily integrated into classrooms (no need to install firewall filters or constantly police online activity, because they're inherently offline). The ministry can also vet all software that runs on them (which would just be the OS and our educational tools), ensuring nothing inappropriate is present - making it culturally and politically palatable to roll out at scale.

Character-building and digital responsibility: One could argue that learning computing in an offline manner first also helps build digital responsibility and skills gradually. Students first learn that computers are tools that they control through programming. Later, when they do get online (in a supervised way at older ages), they hopefully approach the internet more as producers, not just consumers. We are implicitly teaching them to be creators (writing their own programs, games, etc.) before turning them loose into the wild of app stores and endless online content. This may instill a healthier relationship with technology. In the controlled environment, success comes from one's own effort and creativity, not from clicking on the next video. This can foster patience, resilience (debugging teaches persistence!), and a growth mindset. All these are educational benefits that go beyond coding skills.

In conclusion, the safety and cultural context argument strongly supports the Zeal Computer and SuperPascal approach. By being offline-first, we remove the well-known negatives of classroom technology use (distraction, dependence on internet, safety risks) while keeping all the positives (interactive learning, instant feedback, engagement through creation). Bulgaria's specific scenario – with impending regulations against open computing for under-16 – makes this project especially timely. It provides a compliant solution that still achieves educational objectives. And for any region, the idea of intentional computing for kids – where the environment is curated for learning – is gaining traction as a countermeasure to the sometimes chaotic introduction of tech in schools. We are essentially creating a "safe sandbox" for young minds to play in the computing universe, before they venture out to the wider digital world. This ensures that when they do step into that world (as adults or near-adults), they do so with strong skills, critical thinking, and a grounded understanding of technology, rather than having been passive consumers of tech from the start.

# 5. Designing the Educational Progression (Curriculum Integration from Age 10 to University)

A core challenge is not just building the hardware and language, but structuring how students will progress through using them over years. We need a clear educational pathway so that skills and knowledge build

coherently from middle school through high school (and even into university). Here's a proposed progression, aligned with developmental stages and the two-tier toolchain:

Ages 10-12: The introduction to programming (Tier-1 Micro-Pascal). At this stage (roughly 4th-6th grade in many systems), students are typically beginners to text-based coding. They will use the Zeal Computer's native environment primarily. The focus is on fundamental concepts: basic syntax, sequential execution, loops (for, while), conditionals (if), and perhaps simple functions/procedures without parameters (or with very simple parameters). The SuperPascal subset here is deliberately limited to avoid anything that isn't concrete. For example, we might disallow or not introduce pointers, dynamic memory, or even complex data types. A student might write programs to solve math puzzles, or a simple guessing game, or to draw a pattern on the screen (leveraging the FPGA graphics via an easy library call like Plot(x,y) or Line(x1,y1,x2,y2)). These tasks teach algorithmic thinking (e.g. using a loop to draw a repeated pattern, or using conditions to branch logic) without needing to know what's happening under the hood. Hardware fundamentals can be introduced gently: since the Zeal is an actual hardware device, a teacher can show, for instance, that memory is limited ("if your array is too big, you run out of memory") in a very tangible way, or demonstrate using an LED or sound output by toggling bits via a provided routine (but not yet have students directly manipulate hardware registers themselves).

This period is also where we incorporate playful learning to hook student interest. We can include educational games or challenges on the Zeal that encourage students to modify code. For instance, a simple pre-written game could be provided and students are tasked to "hack" it (change the speed, or the colors, by altering variables and seeing effect). This gives a sense of control and discovery. At ages 10-12, concrete projects like "animate a sprite on screen" or "simulate a simple science experiment (e.g. population growth) with code" work well. These tie into other subjects too (STEAM integration), making computing less siloed. It's important that at this stage the curriculum does not overwhelm with theory. We teach by example and practice primarily. Concepts like "CPU", "compiler", or "variable type" can be introduced in an intuitive manner (perhaps not even using formal terms initially - e.g., explain a variable as a "slot in memory where we keep a number", using an analogy kids get).

Ages 13-15: Deepening understanding and a peek under the hood. By middle school (7th-9th grade), students should be comfortable writing small programs in the Tier-1 environment. Now we can start to broaden their SuperPascal subset knowledge and also carefully introduce some computer architecture concepts to satisfy curiosity and lay groundwork. In Pascal terms, this is when we introduce procedures with parameters and return values, the concept of variable scope (global vs local), perhaps user-defined data types like records (so they can bundle related data). Exception handling still likely unnecessary at this level; instead, emphasize error checking via conditional logic (which reinforces their programming basics).

One scaffolding strategy here is to gradually move from purely concrete tasks to more abstract ones. For example, earlier they drew a fixed square on screen with specific coordinates. Now challenge them to write a procedure DrawSquare(size) that can draw a square of any size at a given position. This introduces abstraction (the code works for any size, not one specific case) in a very visual/tangible way. They start thinking in terms of general solutions, which is a big step in algorithmic thinking.

In these years, we can also start teaching about the machine gently. Perhaps around age 14, we can unveil a bit of how the Z80 works. One approach is the use of a simplified visual CPU simulator or by leveraging the Zeal's ability to show low-level state. For instance, we could provide a special "step mode" on the Zeal where students can step through their Pascal program and see registers and memory in a

simplified display. This ties into understanding how high-level code translates to operations. We wouldn't necessarily have them write assembly yet, but we might show them "this is what the CPU is doing when your program runs." The famous "Little Man Computer" analogy (a teaching tool to simulate a simple CPU using a person moving numbers in boxes) could be implemented on Zeal to show how instructions execute. The goal is to make them comfortable with the idea that there is a concrete process underneath their abstract code. By doing so, when they later learn assembly or pointers, it will click ("Oh, that's how we manually manipulate what we used to let the compiler handle").

Project-based learning at this stage can ramp up in complexity. Group projects might be introduced: e.g., two students together program a more complex game or an animation story. They learn to break a problem into modules (one handles graphics, one handles logic), reflecting software engineering fundamentals in a kid-friendly way. The teacher or curriculum should provide a structured path - possibly a sequence of modules that each build on the previous. For example, a syllabus might go: basics of Pascal -> controlling flow -> drawing and coordinates -> simple game logic -> arrays and data -> an array to track scores or positions in a game -> etc. By the end of middle school, a motivated student might make something like a simple tile-based maze game or a basic physics simulation (think of those classic Logo physics projects) on the Zeal. They would have unknowingly used concepts of data structures (array for the maze grid), algorithm (some path finding or movement rules), and perhaps simple debugging of logic.

Crucially, around the end of this phase (age 15), we can start preparing them for Tier-2. This might involve introducing the notion of a compiler vs. interpreter explicitly and explaining that so far, they used a certain kind of compiler that was small and ran on the device. Perhaps we demonstrate (if not already) how limited the micro-compiler is by trying a program that's too large or using a feature it doesn't support, and then motivating: "What if we had a more powerful compiler that runs on a bigger computer? Then we could do X, Y, Z." This creates a narrative for the transition to the cross-compiler. It's important that students don't feel the micro-compiler was "baby stuff" to be thrown away – rather, it was appropriate for their level, and now they are graduating to a more advanced tool, just as they do in math from arithmetic to algebraic tools. We'll maintain continuity in the language so it feels like leveling up in a game, not switching games entirely.

Ages 16–18: Transition to Tier-2 and full-fledged computer science concepts. Typically, these are high school years (10th–12th grade). At around age 16 (perhaps coinciding with 10th or 11th grade), students can be introduced to the SuperPascal cross-compiler running on a PC (or more powerful host). The transition should be orchestrated, possibly as part of a curriculum milestone. For example, one could imagine a Grade 10 course called "Advanced Programming with SuperPascal" where the first assignment is "take one of your earlier programs and compile it with the new compiler – notice it still works, but now you can do more." This demonstrates backward compatibility and builds trust in the continuity of the language.

Now the curriculum shifts from primarily learning to program toward learning computer science and software engineering. With the full language available, we can introduce: object-oriented programming (OOP) – classes, inheritance, polymorphism; data structures – linked lists, stacks, maybe binary trees; algorithms – sorting, searching, basic algorithm analysis (Big-O for instance). Pascal is well-suited for teaching these classical CS topics (Pascal was used for decades in AP Computer Science courses and university intro courses specifically because it handles structured data and algorithms nicely). We will also cover more system-level topics: for instance, pointers and dynamic memory (e.g. how to create a linked list node with a pointer to next). The students are now cognitively ready to handle that indirection and abstraction, and they have the concrete machine understanding from earlier to conceptualize what a pointer might be (an address in memory).

Given the cross-compiler's abilities, students can work on larger projects. Group software projects (with say 3-5 team members) can be done, mirroring real-world dev practices: they might design a more complex game or an application (perhaps a mini "social network" that runs locally across multiple Zeal machines connected in a small network – if networking is available – or a data processing tool). They can learn about organizing code into multiple units/modules, using a version control system (this could be introduced in late high school to prepare for industry practices), and testing/debugging systematically. For example, one could incorporate a unit testing framework (even a simple one we create for SuperPascal) and have students write tests for their code – instilling the concept of software quality.

Architecture and low-level understanding also deepen here. This is where a formal "Computer Architecture" or "Operating Systems" module could be introduced, especially for those interested (maybe an elective or part of a CS track in upper high school). With the Zeal hardware, students can learn about the Z80's instruction set. We could have them write some assembly code for the first time, perhaps as an extension or for extra credit: for instance, write an assembly routine to compute something faster and call it from Pascal (learning about ABIs and calling conventions in the process). Or they could implement a device driver for a new peripheral on the FPGA (if the system allows customization) – for example, if a sensor or external device is connected, write low-level code to read it. These kinds of projects teach about memory-mapped I/O, interrupts, etc., which are classic topics in an advanced high school or early university systems class. Because they're doing it on a simple 8-bit machine, the concepts are easier to see in totality (a modern x86 PC is too complex to fully grasp, but a Z80 with an FPGA video is manageable to mentally model). By end of high school, a truly advanced student in this program might have, for example, built a simple cooperative operating system for the Zeal (with tasks and a scheduler), or written a Pascal compiler for a subset language of their own design, or created a game that pushes the hardware to its limits. These capstone-style experiences are incredibly enriching and mirror what specialized computing magnet schools or clubs might do.

Preparing for university CS: For those going on to university CS, this pipeline would give them a huge head start. But we also ensure the curriculum does not isolate them from mainstream technology. That's a pitfall to avoid: if they only know Pascal on an 8-bit, will they struggle when faced with Python or Java on a Mac in college? We anticipate this by possibly introducing a brief comparative lesson late in high school, showing "here's how the same program looks in Python or C", to illustrate different syntax and encourage adaptability. However, as one educator noted, "if you teach kids the basic concepts in programming, and they've spent enough time coding in one language, they can switch languages later as needed". In fact, the specific first language is less important than the conceptual foundation. Many of us learned on Pascal and then moved to C, Java, etc. So long-term, these students should adapt fine. Nonetheless, part of the high-school curriculum could involve a unit on "other languages and tools" - maybe give them a taste of something like Python to script data, or C to interface with embedded, or even HTML/CSS for web, just to show the landscape. But these would be enrichment topics; the core learning and proficiency remains in the SuperPascal/Zeal ecosystem, which by now has taught them general skills (problem decomposition, debugging, algorithmic thinking) that are transferable.

University-level use of SuperPascal: The question includes using the full-featured compiler at university level as well. Indeed, universities could adopt this for their CS1/CS2 courses (intro programming and data structures). If students come in already knowing it from high school, the university could either transition them to a new language (common in first year – often to C/C++ or Java) or continue with Pascal if they see fit. The advantage of continuing is that students would hit the ground running and could tackle deeper topics immediately. For example, a first-year course could quickly review Pascal basics (since they learned

them in high school) and then move to advanced data structures or even implementing parts of the compiler. By second year, they might implement an actual compiler or portions of one – perhaps writing a simple compiler that generates Z80 assembly, which would be an excellent hands-on project for a compilers class (they could target the Zeal itself, closing the loop!). Another possibility is using the Zeal environment in a Computer Organization course: having students write assembly and C (Pascal) to run on actual 8-bit hardware gives a much more visceral understanding than using a simulator for MIPS or something, as often done. The low cost of hardware means each student or team could have their own “mini computer” to experiment with, rather than just hypothetical examples.

From an educational systems perspective, to integrate this pipeline formally, we would develop a spiral curriculum – concepts are revisited with increasing depth. For example, the concept of “loop” appears in year 1 as a simple repeat mechanism; in year 3 as perhaps an opportunity to discuss loop invariants or efficiency; and by year 5 or 6 (university) in the context of algorithm optimization and maybe even machine-level loop unrolling. Similarly, “variables” start as an intuitive idea, later become a topic of type systems and memory layout, and eventually lead to discussions of stack vs heap allocation. This spiral ensures continuity and reinforcement.

Assessments would also evolve along the progression: initially very practical (write a program to do X), later including design (design an algorithm before coding), and finally theoretical questions (prove correctness, analyze complexity, etc.). By having a consistent platform, students can even revisit their own earlier work with more knowledge - a powerful metacognitive experience. A high-school senior might look back at a program they wrote at 13 and analyze its style or efficiency, learning from how far they've come.

Introducing advanced topics progressively: Some specific advanced topics to time appropriately: - Recursion: Often taught around mid-high school. Pascal supports recursion; we should introduce it perhaps around age 15 when mathematical maturity allows thinking of a function calling itself. Initially with simple examples (factorial, Fibonacci, or the classic Towers of Hanoi puzzle). This also ties to understanding of the call stack which could be visualized on the Zeal in a small scale. Recursion is both a concept and a chance to discuss why one might prefer iteration for efficiency (opening discussion on algorithm complexity). - Exception handling: Likely introduced in late high school when building larger programs that need robust error management. Could be taught alongside events like file I/O (e.g., how to handle if a file not found – earlier they might just assume the file exists, later we show try/except to catch that). We ensure they understand the cost/benefit of exceptions and when to use them. - Polymorphism and design patterns: Once inheritance and virtual methods are taught (probably 11th grade), we can show how to design systems that are extensible – an intro to software design principles. For instance, make a game where different enemy types are classes derived from a base class, showing polymorphism in action. This is not just language feature learning but thinking in terms of abstractions which is crucial for larger software development. - Memory management and the heap: When pointers and new / dispose (or whatever memory allocation mechanism we allow) are taught (likely around 11th grade), it's a perfect time to connect back to computer architecture: show how the heap is just another region of memory. Possibly introduce a tool to watch memory usage or even a simple heap visualization. We should also teach safe practices here (Pascal helps by not having pointer arithmetic in the same wild way as C, but still dangling pointers or leaks can occur if not careful). Students could even build a rudimentary garbage collector as an advanced project, which ties in theory (graph traversal) with system (memory). - Compiler internals: For those interested (maybe an elective in 12th grade or first year uni), use SuperPascal's compiler design as a teaching aid. Since our cross-compiler is written in Rust, we might not dive into it in high school, but we can certainly teach what a compiler does. Possibly have students modify or extend the micro-compiler (which might be

simpler and could even be implemented in Pascal itself for educational purposes). For example, add a new keyword or optimization as a project – this gives a taste of language development and reinforces knowledge of both high-level and machine-level details.

Alignment with educational standards: We should ensure that by the end of secondary education, students have met or exceeded the competencies outlined in national/international standards for CS. Based on CSTA and similar frameworks, by Grade 12 students should be able to: break down complex problems, use multiple programming paradigms, understand data structures & algorithms, and have a mental model of how computer systems work (including networks)  $^{23}$ $^{24}$ . Our curriculum addresses these systematically: - Problem decomposition and abstraction from day one, scaling up through OOP design. - Data structures and algorithms in later years. - Understanding of systems (CPU, memory, simple OS concepts) via the direct use of the hardware and occasional assembly. - Even network concepts could be introduced: though Zeal is offline by design for younger kids, perhaps by 11th grade one can allow controlled network exercises (like linking a few Zeals in a closed LAN to demonstrate communication protocols or client-server concept in a contained way - since older teens can be allowed limited network under supervision). This would satisfy learning about how computers communicate (an important modern skill).

In implementing this progression, teacher training will be critical (discussed in section 7, but relevant here too). Teachers need guidance on how to go from basic coding lessons to more advanced CS topics using the same platform. We would likely create a curriculum guide or even a certification for educators on using SuperPascal at various levels.

Finally, consider motivation and retention: by structuring the learning in increasing complexity, we aim to keep students challenged but not frustrated. Younger kids have fun drawing and making simple games; middle schoolers enjoy controlling something "real" and feeling like hackers; high schoolers appreciate building things of real complexity (maybe even things they can show off, like a game or a tool that others can use). This fosters a pipeline where students remain engaged year after year – which is key to building a strong cohort of future engineers or simply computationally literate citizens. A well-designed progression means by the time they graduate high school, they not only have knowledge but a portfolio of projects and considerable confidence in tackling tech problems, having essentially grown up with the Zeal/ SuperPascal as their training ground.

In summary, the educational progression is a carefully staged journey: Discover -> Learn -> Deepen -> Master -> Innovate. With the two-tier system smoothly supporting each stage (and overlapping in mid-teen years), students gradually evolve from novices typing their first loops into young experts who understand not only how to code, but how the code runs on a machine, and how to design complex systems of their own.

# 6. System Design Implications: Maintaining a Unified Language and Ecosystem

Designing SuperPascal and the surrounding toolchain for two different compilers (micro and full) raises important system design considerations. We must ensure that despite the differing capabilities of the two compilers, SuperPascal remains one coherent language – both in syntax and semantics. Additionally, we need to design the overall ecosystem (IDEs, libraries, testing frameworks) to serve both educational tiers

without causing confusion or incompatibility. This section addresses how to achieve consistency and long-term compatibility, as well as the tooling and infrastructure required to support students and teachers.

One language, two compilers: A guiding principle is that a program written in the subset should compile and run under the full compiler, producing the same results (barring resource constraints like memory). This means the language definition for the subset is literally a subset of the full language definition - not a divergent dialect. For instance, if the subset doesn't support classes, that's fine; but any construct it does support (say, Pascal's case statement or the scope rules for variables) must behave identically in the full compiler. We will maintain a single language specification document that clearly marks which features are Tier-1 and which are Tier-2. During design, whenever we consider modifying or extending Pascal (for modernization), we ensure that either the feature is something that can exist only in Tier-2 with no effect on Tier-1 (e.g., maybe exception handling syntax is simply not recognized by micro-compiler), or if it affects core syntax, the micro-compiler at least can ignore it or handle a trivial subset of it. An example: suppose in full SuperPascal we introduce a String type (dynamically sized string class) for convenience. The microcompiler likely can't implement a full dynamic string library. We have choices: either disallow String in subset (and if a kid tries to use it, micro-compiler errors out with "not supported in this mode" message), or provide a very limited version (maybe a fixed-length string or simply treat it as alias for an array of char with some limitations). We would opt for clarity: it might be better to omit certain features entirely in subset rather than include a crippled form that behaves differently, because that could confuse the mental model. In general, any divergence in semantics is dangerous - it fragments the mental model of the language. We want a student to think in one consistent model of SuperPascal. So features not implementable in micro will just be absent there, not changed.

To guard this, we might implement a common test suite for language conformance. For example, dozens of small sample programs that use each feature, which we run on both compilers to ensure they produce the same output. If any discrepancy arises (say, the micro-compiler's handling of integer overflow differs from the full compiler's), we would treat that as a bug and resolve it. This effort is akin to how modern languages ensure different implementations are consistent via a specification and test suites.

Long-term compatibility: We aim for SuperPascal to be viable and stable for 10-20 years. This means we should be cautious about drastic changes once deployed. The language design committee (including us, presumably) might have many ideas for future features (generics? concurrency? etc.), but we must evaluate them against the need for stability. Educational languages benefit from stability: curriculum materials, once written, should not need constant updates due to language changes. Many recall that Turbo Pascal code from the 80s still compiles in Free Pascal today – that kind of backward compatibility is valuable. So, any new features should be additive and not breaking existing code. If we do add something major (say, we decide to add a simple form of generic programming to full SuperPascal), we can do it in a way that older code still works and subset code is unaffected (likely generics would be a Tier-2 only concept anyway).

We also need to maintain compatibility between the two tiers in terms of binary interface and libraries across versions. For instance, suppose a school writes a set of utility libraries in SuperPascal for students (maybe a library for robotics control in a physics class). Those should be usable whether compiled with micro or full compiler (depending on size constraints). One approach is to standardize the ABI (calling conventions, data type sizes, etc.) from the start. If both compilers use, say, 16-bit integers, 32-bit reals, the same alignment, and call functions using the same register/stack protocol, then an object file compiled by the full compiler can be linked with one compiled by the micro-compiler. We should strive for that consistency. It might be that the micro-compiler doesn't support separate linking at all (for simplicity), but at

least the idea is that if we compiled two modules with the cross-compiler, a micro-compiled main program could call into those modules if we load them together. Another scenario: A student writes a cool program on the cross-compiler (with advanced features). Could we run it on the Zeal using a runtime that the micro environment understands? Ideally yes – as long as it doesn't exceed hardware limits. In practice, we may provide a unified loader on the Zeal that can load binaries produced by either compiler (since they target same machine code format). Fragmentation would occur if, say, the cross-compiler output expects an OS or runtime not present on the Zeal; we avoid that by making sure it targets the same simple runtime environment.

Shared runtime library: We will have a set of standard libraries (for I/O, math, graphics, etc.). It's best if both tiers use the same library code where possible. For example, a WriteLn procedure for console output - the microcompiler might inline a simple routine or call a ROM function. The crosscompiler could link against a library that does the same. If they are different implementations, one might handle something slightly differently (like formatting) which can confuse. So we'll unify them. Perhaps the microcompiler has a minimal subset of the library in ROM, and the crosscompiler, when targeting Zeal, knows to call those ROM routines for consistency (this is how some embedded compilers work - they target firmware routines). Alternatively, we distribute the runtime sources to be compiled for both. For maintainability, a single source for library routines is ideal.

This has toolchain implications: if the micro-compiler is part of the Zeal's ROM, updating its library means updating ROM (which is possible via FPGA reflash if needed, but not ideal frequently). Meanwhile, the crosscompiler's libraries can be updated easily on PC side. We likely design the micro-compiler's library as a subset that rarely needs changes, and any growth in library happens on cross-compiler side with new modules that micro doesn't use. That isolates the core that must remain stable on device.

IDE and user experience continuity: The Tier-1 IDE on the Zeal might be a simple text editor with basic features (maybe no syntax highlighting due to memory, or very limited). The Tier-2 environment on PC could be far richer (full IDE with debugging, etc.). We should try to keep the workflow conceptually similar so students are not lost. For example, on Zeal they press a key to compile/run; on PC IDE, they click a similar button. The messages and error format should be similar (a familiar friend - e.g., "Error at line X: syntax error" style that they recognize, though the cross-compiler can provide more detail like pointing to the token). It might be wise to intentionally not make the PC IDE too fancy at first - maybe begin with a simple custom editor that mimics the look of the Zeal's, then allow advanced students to move to a more professional editor/IDE as needed. This is a design decision between familiarity and taking advantage of PC capabilities. One compromise is to provide a plugin for a popular editor (VS Code, for instance) for SuperPascal, but also provide a "simple mode" editor. Perhaps in the transition phase, teachers could allow either but encourage the simpler one until students get used to the new compiler's features, then those interested can adopt VS Code or a more advanced environment for convenience.

Avoiding fragmentation of community/resources: If the language split into two dialects, you'd end up with separate teaching materials ("MicroPascal for Kids" vs "Advanced Pascal for Teens"). We want instead that there's one set of resources, with sections marked "skip this until you use the full compiler" for younger ones. For example, a single official SuperPascal textbook could be used for 5 years: the early chapters cover everything the micro-compiler can do and are used in middle school; later chapters delve into objects, etc., which are used in high school. This way, students see it's all one continuum. The same goes for online resources, examples, etc. If a student finds a SuperPascal example online (in a controlled offline repository perhaps), if it's too advanced the micro-compiler will just not compile it but they'll soon learn that's because

it uses features they'll learn later. This is better than the code being entirely incompatible or written in a different style. We should encourage coding style guidelines that remain consistent across tiers (for instance, use Pascal's structured style, no reliance on hacks or "undefined behavior" – Pascal by design has less of that than C which is good).

Performance considerations and fragmentation: One interesting system design point is performance differences. Code compiled with the micro-compiler may be less optimized, but on small programs that's fine. However, when students start writing bigger programs on Tier-1 towards the end of its use, they might hit performance limits (like a complex computation running slow). Rather than them getting frustrated, this can be a teachable moment – we can say "the Z80 is slow for this task; how might we make it faster?" They might think algorithmically or realize perhaps the cross-compiler's optimized output would help. Indeed, one can show them that compiling the same code with the full compiler yields faster runtime. This actually demonstrates real-world concepts like optimization, and why compilers matter. It's okay if Tier-1 is slower, as long as it's sufficient for the scale of tasks at that level, and they have an upgrade path if needed. We must just ensure any incompatible optimization doesn't slip in (e.g. if the full compiler used an extended instruction set or non-Z80 architecture by accident – it won't, since we target Z80 specifically).

Interoperability between micro and full outputs: We touched on linking and ABI, but consider also mixed-tier usage. Could a teacher compile a library with the full compiler and give it to students using microcompiler to use? Perhaps, if micro could link external binaries. That would require the micro environment to have a linkerloader. If not, an alternative is providing source and letting micro-compiler compile it (assuming it's subset-compatible). But if the library uses advanced features, that won't compile on micro. So maybe it's better to restrict such cross-tier sharing to when the teacher can supply compiled modules. This is advanced use-case; not needed early on. But by high school, one might want to use micro-compiled code for some real-time tasks and full-compiled for heavy logic - but given both run on same hardware, it's simpler to just use full compiler for everything when available. So probably no need to mix in one program, except possibly if memory constraints push one to compile some parts differently (but that's unlikely - full compiler can also do smaller programs fine).

Toolchain integration for testing and continuous feedback: In an educational setting, having ways to test and verify student code is useful (for assignments, contests, etc.). We can design a testing framework that works across both tiers. For example, a simple unit test library: in full Pascal one can reflect or introspect a bit or at least call test functions easily. In micro, maybe not as dynamic. But one can write test harness programs. We could provide a test runner that runs on PC, but executes student code on an emulated or actual Zeal to check correctness. This kind of infrastructure is more for teachers - to automatically grade or ensure exercises are done right (some countries have automated judging in programming classes). Making sure the output format and run environment are consistent aids in that. E.g., if both compilers produce an Intel HEX or some binary format that can be loaded into an emulator, a test script can just run either compiled result under an emulator and check output. So defining a common executable format for Zeal (like a simple flat binary with a header perhaps) is part of system design. The micro-compiler in memory likely produces code at a fixed location; the cross-compiler can produce relocatable code with a small stub or something. We unify these so that an ".exe" for Zeal is the same concept regardless of compiler origin.

Maintenance and evolution of compilers: From an engineering team perspective (the language design and compiler implementation team), having two compilers means double effort unless we find ways to reuse components. Perhaps the front-end (parser) can be shared. Maybe the micro-compiler can actually

be derived from the cross-compiler's front-end but with a different back-end that emits directly to memory and a simpler optimizer. Or vice versa: micro-compiler might be written in Pascal or C and very standalone for simplicity on device. If they are completely separate codebases, we risk them diverging in accepted syntax or quirks. For example, one might accept a construct the other flags as error. To prevent that, we could ensure that the language spec is strictly followed by both. Possibly writing a lot of conformance tests (like mentioned) is one solution. Another approach: implement micro-compiler as a subset of the cross-compiler's code, maybe cross-compiling the cross-compiler to run on Z80 – but a Rust-based compiler is far too heavy to run on Z80. Alternatively, maintain a single EBNF grammar file and generate two compilers from it with certain options toggled. These are technical strategies to reduce divergence. In absence of that, rigorous specification and testing is the way.

Hardware/FPGA implications: The Zeal's FPGA subsystem likely provides graphics and sound, possibly other IO. We should design the interface (memory-mapped registers, etc.) such that it's straightforward to use from Pascal. Perhaps a Graphics unit with procedures that internally do PORT[...] operations or call BIOS routines. Both compilers should use the same approach to driving hardware (e.g., both call the same addresses or OS routines). If down the line the hardware changes (say Zeal 2.0 with improved graphics), we might update the library but keep backward compatible in interfaces. That's a systems design point: decouple student code from direct hardware assumptions by providing library abstraction, but not so much abstraction that they never learn hardware. We want them to eventually peek under the covers. The solution: provide libraries but also teach advanced students how those libraries work (perhaps open source them). That way, advanced students can drop to writing directly to hardware addresses if needed (like writing an assembly ISR for a timer).

Ensuring durability of platform: To last 10-20 years, the system design must accommodate future tech integration. For instance, if in 10 years AI assistance is acceptable in class (contrary to now), could our platform integrate it in a controlled way? Possibly by adding an offline AI trained on the curriculum to assist. We aren't focusing on that now, but leaving hooks (like an ability to load "hint" or code templates from a secure source) might be something to consider. Also, if a new peripheral becomes educationally relevant (say a certain sensor or robotics extension), the language or library might extend to handle it. That shouldn't break old stuff, just add new units (like adding a Robot unit with classes to control a robot - micro might not support classes, so maybe it just has procedures if needed).

Cultural and language design considerations: We should ensure SuperPascal remains "safe" (in terms of not allowing operations that could corrupt the system or lead students astray). Pascal by nature is quite safe (no arbitrary memory access without going through typed pointers, etc.), but our design choices matter. For example, do we allow inline assembly for advanced users? If we do (like how some Pascal compilers do via an assembler block), that's powerful but dangerous in untrained hands. Perhaps allow it only in Tier-2 (and even then only in a mode). Similarly, pointer arithmetic and casting - original Pascal was strict, but many modern Pascals allow some casting for systems work. We might restrict such unsafe operations to library/ internal code, not encourage in student code, to maintain a "safe language for schools" reputation (as policy makers like languages that prevent students from accidentally writing self-replicating viruses or hacking the system, etc.). This also means maybe sandboxing what student programs can do: since it's a bare-metal system, a misbehaving program can hang the Zeal. That's not a big deal (just reboot), but it means multi-user issues are moot because one machine per student presumably. There's no "security" needed beyond that.

Tooling for instructors: The system should provide tools for teachers to easily load code onto devices, collect code, and diagnose issues. For instance, a classroom might have dozens of Zeals – a way to deploy a new FPGA image or library update to all of them easily (maybe via a master USB or something) should be in place. Or a simulator on teacher's PC to demonstrate something on projector. We can develop a Zeal emulator that runs on PC, integrated with the cross-compiler's IDE, so teachers or students at home can test things without physical hardware (but of course, the real hardware is the ultimate target in class). This emulator should mimic cycles enough that performance differences are known. It's easier to maintain one emulator than, say, rely on each student's program being tested only on device.

Sustainability of ecosystem: We will consider open-sourcing parts of the system (perhaps the crosscompiler and libraries) to encourage community contribution and longevity. However, we must also maintain quality control to prevent divergence or forks that cause confusion in schools. Possibly a governance model where the Ministry or an academic board controls the official SuperPascal standard and releases.

In short, the systems design must ensure the unity of language and experience across different levels of complexity. By careful specification, consistent libraries/ABI, integrated toolchains, and planning for compatibility, we can create an ecosystem that feels seamless as the student grows. We want them to think of one continuous journey in the same world, not a jump to a new world mid-way. With that, we avoid the common pitfall where an introductory "toy" environment is discarded in favor of a professional one – instead, our intro environment evolves into the professional one as the student's skills evolve. This continuity is a key selling point to curriculum developers, who prefer not to retrain students on a new language midstream if possible. And if eventually students move to, say,  $C++$  or Python in higher ed, they will still see the through-line from what they did with SuperPascal (structured programming, algorithms, data structures) to the new language's way of doing things – all because we preserved clear semantics and good practices all along.

# 7. Policy, Curriculum Adoption, and Long-Term Sustainability

For the SuperPascal and Zeal Computer initiative to thrive over 10-20 years, it must align with educational policy requirements, be adopted into curricula, and remain sustainable in terms of community and support. This final section addresses how ministries of education and curriculum boards evaluate such proposals, what makes a language/platform "safe" and appealing for school use, and how to ensure longevity (both technical and programmatic) so that the effort continues to deliver value for decades.

Alignment with educational objectives and standards: Education ministries will scrutinize how this ecosystem maps to their curriculum standards. We need to clearly articulate how SuperPascal helps achieve learning outcomes in computing and beyond. Many countries now treat computing as a fundamental literacy, and have standards for algorithmic thinking, programming, hardware knowledge, etc. In Bulgaria, for example, there may be specific syllabi for Informatics (CS) in middle and high school. We should demonstrate that our two-tier approach can cover everything in those syllabi: e.g., problem solving, data representation, control structures, etc., in middle school; and abstract data types, algorithms, and basic systems concepts in high school. The advantage of controlling our own language is we can shape it to teach exactly those concepts in a clear way. For instance, if the curriculum expects students to learn about "procedural vs object-oriented programming", we have both modes in the same language to compare and teach. If the curriculum expects understanding of memory, our hardware focus covers that. This comprehensive coverage can be a selling point. Additionally, beyond CS class, the Zeal Computer can be

integrated into physics or math classes (e.g., using it to collect data from sensors in a physics experiment or to simulate math problems), showing cross-curricular value. Ministries appreciate when a platform is versatile across subjects, maximizing return on investment.

Safety and appropriateness for schools: We earlier discussed the offline and safe nature from a child-development perspective. From a policy perspective, "safe" also means minimizing risks and liabilities for the school. Using open source, well-audited software and hardware designs ensures there are no hidden functions (like some device secretly logging students, etc.). The system has no connectivity, so no risk of students accessing banned content. Also, "safe" in a pedagogical sense: Pascal is strongly typed and encourages structured programming, which prevents many common programming errors. It has no dangerous constructs like unchecked memory access that could lead to bizarre bugs; as an education-focused language, it "fails fast" with errors when something is wrong rather than, say, letting an array out-of-bounds access corrupt memory silently. This is important because it teaches students in a disciplined way. (Recall the earlier point that Pascal programs "crashed much less" on early micros due to these safety features 43.) A language considered pedagogically safe by committees often has these traits: readable syntax, encourages good style, catches errors, and is not easily misused to do malicious things. SuperPascal checks those boxes. It has a lineage from Wirth's Pascal which was explicitly for teaching problem solving and reliable programming.

Ministries will also ask: is this platform inclusive and accessible? We should highlight that text-based coding (with potentially localization of keywords if needed – though likely we keep English keywords, but you can have error messages or docs in the native language) is accessible to all students as long as they can type. The curriculum might also consider accommodations for students with disabilities. For example, could a visually impaired student use this? Possibly via a braille terminal or screen reader output – we might consider providing a mode where the system outputs text that a screen reader can interpret (maybe more relevant on PC side). The hardware itself should be robust and kid-proof to some extent (keys that can't be easily popped out, etc.), or if it's more of a kit, ensure it's safe (no exposed dangerous voltages, etc.). All these factors contribute to administrators feeling it's "safe" to deploy widely.

Cost and resource evaluation: A ministry will weigh the cost of adopting this system versus alternatives. We should provide a cost-effective plan: the Zeal Computer presumably is cheaper than a full PC (perhaps significantly so, especially if mass-produced). Basic components like a Z80 or equivalent, an FPGA, etc., can be made affordable, especially when scaled and using modern inexpensive FPGAs. We could mention that it's likely cheaper per seat than providing laptops, and also cheaper over time because the software doesn't need expensive licenses (it's all custom or open source). Additionally, lower power usage and longer lifespan (the hardware does not need frequent replacement if it's not running heavy processes - an 8-bit device doesn't become "obsolete" in terms of requiring more power for the same tasks, since tasks remain the same). A 20-year pipeline implies that ideally the devices issued now can still function and run the same educational software in  $10+$  years (like how in some developing countries they still use legacy computers for basic programming classes because the content hasn't changed - e.g., using decade-old PCs for Python classes). The Zeal could have longevity because it's not chasing performance; as long as it doesn't physically break, it's sufficient for its role.

We should also consider teacher training costs. We would provide professional development sessions and materials to get teachers up to speed. Pascal might not be known to younger teachers (who likely learned Java or Python in their education), but it's a straightforward language to pick up, especially the subset. We can highlight that many experienced teachers might have familiarity with Pascal (given it was widely taught

in the 80s/90s), which could be nostalgic and beneficial. And for new teachers, we can emphasize that teaching with Pascal/Zeal has advantages such as immediate feedback and no distractions, which actually make classroom management easier (no internet to monitor, fewer unpredictable environment issues).

Ministries also love pilot data and research evidence. Over the next 2 years (as timeline suggests), we could run pilot programs in a few schools and measure outcomes: e.g., improved exam scores in computational thinking, higher engagement in class, etc. If positive, that evidence would be used to justify broader adoption. We should plan for such pilot studies in collaboration with educational researchers, gathering both quantitative results and qualitative feedback from students and teachers.

Modern vs industry relevance: A potential concern a curriculum board might raise: "Why Pascal? Isn't that outdated? Shouldn't we teach Python or something mainstream?" We must be ready to address this. The core argument is that for foundational learning, the specific language matters far less than how well it instills proper concepts and habits 42 . As one teacher noted, languages come and go, but teaching kids how to think and solve problems is the main goal 7 . Pascal is an excellent pedagogical vehicle for that. Once they know how to program well in Pascal, they can pick up any new language readily. In fact, Pascal arguably forms a better foundation than starting with Python (which, while easy, is dynamically typed and doesn't enforce structure, possibly leading to more trial-and-error coding and less understanding of types and memory). We can cite that historically, many top computer scientists and engineers learned with Pascal or similar, and it did not hinder them - indeed it helped them. We can also mention that some modern languages (like Ada/SPARK, or even parts of Rust) share Pascal's emphasis on safety and clarity, so we're not at all off-track. Moreover, there's a cultural component: using a "retro" computing approach can be motivational (the novelty of it) and avoids the complexity bloat of modern environments.

For these reasons, we argue that SuperPascal is a deliberate choice for education, not meant to be what they use professionally later, but to prepare them for any language. A quote we might include: "...teaching good programming habits trumps teaching a popular language." 44 . That sentiment resonates with educators who understand that fundamentals are key.

Ministry evaluation of platform will also consider if this is something that can be integrated into the existing curriculum without too much disruption. Ideally, we show how it can slot into current classes. For example, in Bulgaria, if Informatics is taught starting grade 8, maybe we propose starting Zeal in grade 5-6 for preinformatics (algorithmic thinking), then formal Informatics at grade 8 using it, etc. We provide sample curricula and lesson plans as part of the package, to lower the barrier for adoption. We might even propose a phased roll-out: e.g., Year 1, do pilots and teacher training; Year 2, introduce in a couple of grades; Year 3, expand to more, etc., with evaluation at each stage.

Language "safety" and longevity: We touched on safety. Another aspect is the risk of the platform becoming obsolete. To guard against that, we ensure it's built on open standards and commonly available components. If Z80 chips ceased production (they haven't yet, and FPGAs can emulate them anyway), we can adapt (maybe move to a 6502 or a soft-core CPU). But all that can be abstracted so from the user side, nothing changes. Another longevity factor: if at some point secondary schools want to move to a different language (like if a directive comes "we must teach more Java because industry says so"), how would that interact with our pipeline? One approach is to eventually allow the cross-compiler to target other languages or interoperate (but that's complex and maybe outside scope; instead, we can emphasize that nothing prevents a student from learning a second language later, and in fact their strong base will make it easier). We might also consider providing, at Tier-2 level, the ability to generate C or interface with C libraries, so

advanced students can experiment with other language ecosystems – but that is icing, not necessary for the core educational mission.

Sustainability of support: For a two-decade horizon, we need an ecosystem of support: this includes a community of practice among teachers (maybe an online forum where they share lesson ideas, code examples), continuous updates/maintenance of the software (fixing any bugs in compilers, adding new library support when needed for new devices), and institutional support. Possibly partnership with universities to maintain the project (e.g., a university CS department might take on stewardship of developing new educational materials or refining the compiler as per feedback). Government backing could fund a small team or leverage open source contributions to keep the platform up-to-date.

One relevant example: the BBC micro:bit project (the modern one) succeeded not only by distributing hardware but by building an ecosystem of educators, online editors, and community contributions. We should emulate that approach - ensure there's an easy way for content (projects, lessons, challenges) to be created and shared. In our offline-first scenario, maybe a curated collection of projects comes with each device (or on an SD card) so students can try out things others made (safe curated content). This encourages exploration and sustainability through interest.

Avoiding pitfalls in technical/curricular design: Finally, let's enumerate some pitfalls to avoid and how to mitigate them, as that will be crucial in long-term success: - Pitfall: Overwhelming beginners with complexity - We avoid this by the two-tier model itself and scaffolding. We must be disciplined in keeping Tier-1 simple. - Pitfall: Boring advanced students or limiting them - Some students might outgrow Tier-1 quickly; we should have enrichment ready (perhaps allow them to use Tier-2 early under teacher's discretion). Also, the language subset, while limited, should still allow creative projects (we should ensure even with limited features, one can do interesting things - e.g., maybe allow simple graphics in Tier-1 to keep it engaging). - Pitfall: Divergent language dialects - already addressed, maintain one language spec and test across compilers. - Pitfall: Teacher resistance or lack of training - We must invest in good teacher training and user-friendly documentation. Show teachers that this is not an extra burden but actually can make their teaching easier (e.g., no need to manage PC lab maintenance or worry about internet misuse). Possibly identify teacher champions from pilot schools who can advocate peer-to-peer. - Pitfall: Lack of university buy-in - If universities or policymakers think "this isn't what we teach at higher level," they might not support high schools using it. We mitigate by involving some university professors in the design (perhaps they co-author the curriculum or see how it feeds into their first-year courses). Also by demonstrating that our graduates come into uni with stronger fundamentals, making their job easier. - Pitfall: Technological obsolescence - ensure hardware can be repaired/upgraded. Possibly design the Zeal in a modular way (if FPGA is core, that can be reprogrammed; if keyboard/display wear out, they can be replaced). Also plan for manufacturing: maybe partner with a local electronics firm or use open hardware model so multiple can produce if needed. - Pitfall: Competition from alternative tools - e.g. someone might propose just using Chromebooks with Scratch for middle school and Python for high school as an easier route. We need to articulate the unique benefits of our approach: true understanding of how computers work, ability to go from low-level to high-level, no internet needed (critical in places pushing for offline), and building a pipeline that fosters engineering mindset from an early age, not just coding-as-usage. Also the excitement factor of having a physical device to work on that they can call their own (pride and sense of ownership). We can even let students personalize their Zeal (case designs, etc.) to build a cultural attachment.

Forward-looking roadmap: To ensure systemic adoption, we propose: 1. Year 0-1: Research and development (largely done concurrently with pilot). Finalize hardware design, develop compilers (with educators testing them), draft curriculum materials. Engage with Ministry early – perhaps get approval for a pilot in select schools. 2. Year 1-2: Pilot in, say, 5-10 schools (urban and rural mix). Train those teachers deeply, monitor classes. Gather data on student engagement and performance. Identify any issues (e.g., if something in language is confusing, or hardware is failing, etc.) and iterate solutions. 3. Year 2: Use pilot success and feedback to refine everything. The Ministry can then prepare to scale – allocate budget for devices, include training in teacher professional dev programs nationwide. 4. Year 3-5: Phased rollout to more schools/year levels. Possibly year 3: all 5th graders get Zeal for computing basics; year 4: extend to grade 6 and 9 (introducing at multiple points); year 5: full deployment across target grades. Provide continuous support through an online helpdesk or community. 5. Year  $5+$ : Evaluate exam results and adjust. For long-term sustainability, consider establishing a National Computing Education Lab in partnership with a university or the Ministry that maintains the platform, updates it as needed (maybe a new version of Zeal after 8-10 years if needed), and keeps content modern. They can also oversee that the program stays relevant to job market needs (though fundamentals rarely change, they can add modules like introducing AI or data science in small doses at appropriate ages, using the base skills from SuperPascal). 6. 10-20 year horizon: The pipeline's first cohort will be in workforce or higher ed by then. Hopefully, success breeds continuity – if those cohorts show superior skills, the program will have justified itself. At that stage, one can think of expanding it further (perhaps downward to primary school or upward into more advanced electives, or exporting the model to other countries with similar educational needs).

Conclusion and multi-stakeholder advice: Summing up for each audience: - Ministry of Education: This approach aligns with your goals of increasing STEM competency while addressing parental concerns about internet and device misuse. It's a safe, controlled, cost-effective solution that builds computational thinking from an early age. We advise supporting a phased implementation and funding for training and devices. The outcome will be a generation of students with not only coding skills but a deep understanding of how computers work - potentially boosting national capacity in software and hardware engineering in the long run. - University CS curriculum board: Incorporating this pipeline means incoming students will have consistent backgrounds in rigorous programming fundamentals. We recommend working with secondary educators to ensure the later stage of SuperPascal (Tier-2) dovetails with university expectations (for instance, by final year of high school students have covered topics equivalent to an intro CS course). You might consider accepting a SuperPascal project as part of admission portfolios or aligning first-year courses to build on the architecture knowledge they gained. We encourage collaboration so that the transition to university is smooth - e.g., perhaps a bridging course that moves students from Pascal on Zeal to C on larger systems, explicitly comparing concepts so they see continuity. - Language design committee: Maintain the integrity and simplicity of SuperPascal. Any enhancements should be driven by clear educational need and tested for classroom viability. Avoid "feature creep" that doesn't serve learning outcomes. Keep in mind the dual-compiler environment in any decision: if a new feature can't run on Z80, ensure it's truly necessary or find a creative way to simulate it for teaching purposes if needed. Continue to gather input from teachers to refine language usability (for instance, if students commonly make a certain mistake, could language or compiler provide a better error message or tweak?). Essentially, treat this language as a living educational tool, not just a static spec - be willing to iterate carefully, but always with backward compatibility in mind. - Engineering implementation team: Focus on robustness and consistency. The micro-compiler should be nearly bug-free and crash-proof (no teacher wants the device freezing because of a compiler bug in front of 30 kids). Test thoroughly under heavy use conditions. For the cross-compiler, prioritize generating correct code over ultra-optimization initially (though some optimization is important for larger programs). Ensure both compilers produce identical results for overlapping features

(lots of cross-testing). Also consider user experience: fast compile times, useful error diagnostics – these can greatly affect how students learn (cryptic errors can discourage, while helpful ones can teach). Also pay attention to toolchain integration – e.g., make it easy to transfer programs from PC to device, perhaps via a single USB cable or memory card, with minimal steps (the less friction, the more class time is spent learning instead of fiddling with tools). Plan for maintainability: use clean modular code, possibly generate parts of compilers from common definitions as noted, and document the system well (so it can be maintained even if original developers move on).

By addressing each stakeholder's needs - educational effectiveness for ministries and teachers, rigorous foundation for academia, and technical soundness for developers - the Zeal Computer and SuperPascal ecosystem can become a cornerstone of computing education in the region. It is a forward-looking yet historically informed project: it takes the best lessons from the golden age of home computing and combines them with modern pedagogical insights to prepare students for the challenges of the 21st-century digital world.

Ultimately, this strategy sets students on a path from curiosity to mastery: tinkering with simple 8-bit programs in childhood, growing into competent software developers by young adulthood, all under a cohesive, nurturing educational framework. It's an investment in human capital that, with careful execution and sustained support, will yield innovators and problem-solvers equipped to advance technology in the country and beyond.
