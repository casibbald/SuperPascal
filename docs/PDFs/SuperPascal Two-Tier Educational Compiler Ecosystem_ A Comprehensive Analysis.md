# SuperPascal Two-Tier Educational Compiler Ecosystem: A Comprehensive Analysis

Introduction

The "Zeal Computer" initiative proposes an offline-first computing ecosystem to teach programming and STEM fundamentals to students without full PCs or internet access. It centers on SuperPascal, a modernized Pascal dialect with two tiers: (1) a Z80-native micro-compiler for ages 10-15 covering a safe subset of the language, and (2) a full-featured cross-compiler (implemented in Rust) for ages  $16+$  that supports advanced features (units, OOP, exceptions, ABI, etc.). This report analyzes the project from historical, pedagogical, technical, cultural, and systemic perspectives. It synthesizes insights to guide a Ministry of Education, a university CS curriculum board, a language design committee, and an engineering team in making informed decisions. We compare past educational computer models, discuss how to scaffold learning from simple to advanced concepts, assess technical feasibility on 8-bit hardware, address safety and policy factors (particularly in Bulgaria's context), and chart a roadmap for implementation. The goal is to ensure SuperPascal's two-tier strategy is educationally sound, technically viable, and sustainable over the long term.

# Historical Precedents for Two-Tier Compiler Models

Early personal computers used in education offer valuable precedents. In the 1980s, schools commonly introduced computing via simple on-device languages and gradually exposed advanced tools as students matured. For example, the BBC Micro in the UK was a landmark educational computer with an easy-to-use built-in BASIC interpreter. By 1985,  $80\%$  of British schools had a BBC Micro thanks to government subsidy and a design blending "geeky" capability with accessibility  $1 \quad 2$ . BBC BASIC was simple enough for beginners (allowing immediate coding at a prompt) yet powerful (it even allowed inline assembly) so that advanced students could push the machine's limits. This balance helped "introduce a generation to computing...urging a nation to make things rather than solely consume"  $3 \quad 4$ . The BBC Micro's success came from simplicity and a focus on fundamentals - it was explicitly designed "to help a generation learn to use and code computers"  $5$ . Its instant-on environment (no complex OS) and BASIC prompt meant even a 10-year-old could write "HELLO WORLD" on day one  $6$ . Such early wins built confidence and enthusiasm. Over a million BBC Micros were sold, seeding the UK's tech workforce and even leading indirectly to technological innovations like the ARM chip  $7 \quad 8$ . The two-tier ethos can be seen here: start with an approachable tool (BASIC on BBC Micro) and later transition to more advanced systems. In fact, the BBC Micro's "spiritual successor," the modern BBC Micro:bit device for schools, follows the same idea - it is "very basic, which is a good thing as teaching kids how to code has to start at the beginning and have a sense of quick success"  $9$ . Early feedback on the Micro:bit showed it "encourages independent learning, gives pupils a strong sense of achievement, and can inspire those not usually interested in computers"  $10$ - underscoring the importance of simplicity and tangible results for beginners.

Other 8-bit platforms of the era also inform our model. Commodore 64 and Apple II computers likewise booted into BASIC, giving novices an immediate low-floor entry. However, they offered pathways to higher

complexity when ready. The Apple II, for instance, had an optional UCSD Pascal system (Apple Pascal) on floppy disk which introduced structured programming in a more robust environment once students outgrew BASIC. UCSD Pascal in the late 1970s was groundbreaking - "arguably the first high-level programming system that worked on small [64KB] machines that schools and individuals could afford", made possible by an innovative portable p-code engine 11. With Apple Pascal, high school or university students could learn structured programming and data structures, even though the Apple II's native BASIC was too limited for those concepts. This mirrors SuperPascal's plan: use the Z80 micro-compiler like the built-in BASIC (fast, simple, one-pass), then let advanced students use the cross-compiler (akin to a more powerful Pascal environment) on a host machine for larger projects.

Turbo Pascal on CP/M (for Z80/8080) and later MS-DOS is another historical touchstone. Created by Anders Hejlsberg in 1983, Turbo Pascal demonstrated that a full-featured language could run on micros with incredibly fast compile times and low resource usage  $^{12}$ $^{13}$ . Turbo Pascal's compiler was a single-pass, native code compiler that generated machine code as it parsed, without building heavy intermediate representations  $^{14}$ . This was possible due to Pascal's language design (e.g. requiring declarations before use)  $^{14}$ . The result: compilation and execution were dramatically faster than contemporaries like C. One practitioner recalls that in the 1980s, a similar-sized program compiled in  $\sim 5$  seconds with Turbo Pascal versus  $\sim 40$  seconds in C on the same hardware  $^{15}$ $^{16}$ . This speed tightened the edit-compile-run loop, which was crucial on slow machines  $^{15}$ . Turbo Pascal also combined editor, compiler, and runtime in one environment, avoiding the need for separate tools and slow disk I/O for linking  $^{17}$ . These lessons are directly relevant: a microcompiler for an 8-bit Z80 should likewise be one-pass and lightweight, providing near-instant feedback to keep young learners engaged. Indeed, the Z80 was regarded as one of the best compiler targets of its era (better than the 6502, which had fewer registers and tricky memory constraints)  $^{12}$ $^{18}$ . Successful products like Turbo Pascal and others proved that structured languages could run within 64KB and still outperform simpler languages (Turbo Pascal produced much faster code than interpreted BASIC, for example). Our Z80 microcompiler can take inspiration from Turbo Pascal 3.0, which implemented a sizeable subset of Pascal on CP/M. Modern retro-computing projects like PASTA/80 (a new open-source Turbo Pascal 3.0 clone for Z80) have validated this approach: PASTA/80's author built it as a single-pass, recursive descent compiler generating Z80 machine code on the fly, trading some code optimality for simplicity and speed  $^{19}$ $^{20}$ . PASTA/80 closely mirrors Turbo Pascal 3.0's feature set (with enhancements like Break, Continue, etc.), showing that a rich subset of Pascal can fit into a Z80's world  $^{21}$ $^{22}$ . Notably, it lacks separate compilation - everything is compiled from source each time - which simplifies the compiler greatly at the cost of larger program binaries  $^{23}$ $^{24}$ . This is an acceptable trade-off in a constrained environment and one we may also adopt for the SuperPascal microcompiler. The takeaway from Turbo Pascal and UCSD Pascal is clear: educational systems in the past often provided a tiered progression - easy start with BASIC or simple Pascal, then a path to more advanced Pascal or other languages as students advanced. They also balanced simplicity vs. power by using language design and innovative compiler techniques to work within tight memory. We aim to revive these proven strategies with modern refinements.

It's also instructive to examine systems that failed or were less effective. The Sinclair ZX Spectrum, wildly popular among 1980s kids (5 million units sold  $^{25}$ ), came with Sinclair BASIC in ROM. ZX BASIC made the Spectrum extremely accessible to beginners, but it had serious limitations: it was very slow and lacked structured programming constructs (no procedures or local variables, only line-numbered GOTO/GOSUB logic)  $^{26}$ . As one commentator wryly noted, "the built-in BASIC made the Spectrum very accessible. But it was also quite limited, mostly due to being very slow"  $^{27}$ . The Spectrum fostered a generation of "bedroom coders" who learned programming, but the unstructured nature of BASIC often led to poor habits. (Computer

scientist Edsger Dijkstra famously quipped that students exposed to BASIC were "mentally mutilated beyond hope of regeneration" in terms of learning good programming 26 .) While that is an extreme view, it underlines a key point: educational languages must be simple without ingraining bad practices. This is a major rationale for SuperPascal's design. BASIC taught millions the joy of coding, but its lack of data types, structure, or safety is not ideal for modern foundational education. SuperPascal's subset intends to be as approachable as BASIC (no overwhelming syntax or requirement to manage complex toolchains), yet it will enforce structured programming (e.g. proper procedures, control structures, strong types) from the start, setting better habits. In essence, we want the low barrier to entry of BASIC with the high ceiling and good practices of Pascal. Projects like the Spectrum4 (a modern ZX Spectrum-inspired educational kit) have recognized the nostalgic appeal of 8-bit BASIC but also its limits - Spectrum4 gives students the flavor of a Spectrum, but educators find BASIC "a little too limiting" for a full curriculum (e.g. insufficient for teaching structured software development). Our approach with SuperPascal addresses that by offering a more robust language on similar hardware.

In summary, historical evidence supports a two-tier model: children begin with a trusted, simple environment on hardware (just as 8-bit micros did in the 80s), then graduate to a more powerful development environment as their skills grow. Past educational systems like BBC Micro and Apple II Pascal show this can be done without overwhelming beginners, and that a well-designed subset language can run even on minimal 8-bit CPUs. We stand on the shoulders of these successes, updating the concept for the 2020s.

# Pedagogical Frameworks and Learning Progression

Teaching programming to children requires careful scaffolding. Research in cognitive science and education indicates that novices face a high intrinsic cognitive load when learning programming due to the abstract and complex nature of coding  $^{28}$ . Inexperienced learners have limited mental models, so if we introduce too many concepts or too much complexity at once, their working memory overloads, impeding learning  $^{29}$ . Thus, effective pedagogy for youth avoids unnecessary complexity and focuses on one new concept at a time, building knowledge incrementally - a process known as scaffolding  $^{30}$ . The idea is to reduce extraneous cognitive load (complex syntax, confusing tools, irrelevant details) so that students can grapple with the intrinsic challenges of programming (like logical reasoning and problem decomposition) without being overwhelmed  $^{31}$ . In practice, this means an educational language for kids should have a minimalist, forgiving syntax, quick feedback loops, and eliminate hazards like manual memory management or confusing error messages. Pascal is well-suited here: it was literally designed by Professor Niklaus Wirth as a teaching language emphasizing clarity and structured control, and its strong typing and simple semantics prevent many "gotchas" that low-level languages have. SuperPascal's subset will be crafted to maximize clarity and safety - for instance, omitting pointers or other advanced features for younger students, and including helpful compile-time errors to catch mistakes.

Why avoid complexity for children? Put simply, younger learners are still developing abstract thinking abilities. Concepts like indirection (pointers), concurrency, or even object-oriented design can be too abstract for a 12-year-old to fully grasp. Developmental psychology (e.g. Piaget's stages) suggests that early adolescents (around 10-14) are transitioning from concrete operational thinking to more abstract formal operational thinking. They can handle concrete, relatable models but may struggle with layers of abstraction without real examples. That is why introductory languages for kids often use very tangible metaphors (e.g. Logo's "turtle graphics") or simple sequential logic. A core principle is to teach general problem-solving and computational thinking first, before introducing intricate language paradigms. Educational research supports introducing fundamental programming concepts in stages: sequence, loops,

and basic conditionals come first (often in late primary or early middle school)  $^{32}$ . Only once those are mastered do we add things like structured decomposition (functions/procedures) and simple data structures, typically in middle school or early high school  $^{33}$ . Object-oriented programming (OOP) and more abstract concepts (like user-defined types, classes, inheritance) are usually introduced in later high school or undergraduate courses. For example, many high school curricula (such as AP Computer Science at age  $\sim 16 - 18$ ) introduce OOP via Java or Python, but only after students know loops, conditionals, and basic data types. It's generally agreed that pre-teens need to focus on the "absolute basics" first – understanding that instructions execute in order, how variables work, and how to formulate simple algorithms  $^{32}$ . One educator noted that children must "get a feel for the relationship between code and actions" with immediate results before tackling abstraction  $^{34}$ . Once they grasp concrete relationships and sequential logic, one can gently introduce modularization (dividing code into subroutines), then encapsulation (grouping data with functions), and eventually the idea of objects and polymorphism  $^{35}$ . This phased approach matches our two-tier compiler model: the Z80 subset for ages  $\sim 10 - 15$  will solidify the basics (procedural programming, variables, control flow, perhaps simple data structures like arrays/records), while the full compiler for  $16+$  will introduce classes, inheritance, interfaces, and more sophisticated type/system concepts appropriate for late high school or university level. By that age, students are better prepared for abstract thinking and can appreciate concepts like exceptions (which require understanding of error propagation and non-linear control flow) or generic types (abstracting over types), which would likely confuse a younger student.

Crucially, cognitive load theory suggests that mastery of simpler schemas frees up mental capacity to learn more complex ones  $36 \quad 37$ . Our curriculum design should therefore ensure that by the time a student transitions from the micro-compiler to the full compiler, they have automated the fundamentals in their mind. For example, a 15-year-old moving up should already be fluent in writing loops and functions so that learning about objects or pointers is one new layer, not a completely foreign exercise. The SuperPascal micro-compiler environment will encourage that fluency through lots of practice on the basics. It should have tools and exercises to drill things like loops, conditional logic, simple algorithms (sorting, searching) and perhaps low-level understanding (like how variables reside in memory, albeit in a simplified form). This builds the mental schema novices need  $38 \quad 39$ . By scaffolded progression, we can align with cognitive development stages: e.g., ages 10-12 might work mostly with concrete scenarios (perhaps controlling simple graphics or robotics via code to see immediate effect), ages 13-15 can handle more abstract tasks (like algorithms, or understanding a simplified CPU model), and ages 16-18 can engage with full-scale programming concepts (like designing classes, reading assembly listings, or understanding compilation).

Another consideration is motivation and engagement. Pedagogically, it's important to avoid discouraging students with overly complex or irrelevant details early on. The offline-first approach actually helps here: in a controlled environment, students won't be distracted by the internet or tempted to just copy-paste solutions. But we must keep the environment itself enticing. Instant feedback and visual or game-like results are great for engagement. Classic systems succeeded by letting students create something visible (like a moving sprite or a sound) with just a few lines of code. SuperPascal on the Zeal Computer, thanks to its FPGA-based graphics/sound, should provide a rich yet accessible creative sandbox. We can include simple graphics libraries in the micro-compiler so that a 12-year-old can, for instance, draw shapes or make a character move with very basic code. This echoes the success of Logo (turtle graphics) and the motivational aspect of BASIC (where printing text or drawing pixels was immediate). The pedagogy of "immediate reward" is key: early success builds confidence, which is essential at pre-teen ages. As the BCS noted about the BBC Micro Bit: quick, tangible success keeps interest high 40 41 .

Advanced concepts absorption timing: Based on both research and observed curricula around the world: - Exceptions and robust error handling: likely not truly understood until  $\sim 16+$  when students can handle the idea of events disrupting normal flow. Before that, simpler error handling (like return codes or basic validations) is more appropriate. We wouldn't burden a 12-year-old with try-catch syntax in their first language; instead, the micro-compiler might just abort on errors or handle them implicitly. In the crosscompiler (for older students), we can introduce exceptions as a more elegant mechanism once they grasp call stacks and conditions. - Object-oriented programming (OOP): Many educators introduce it in late high school. It involves abstraction (modeling real-world entities in code, inheritance hierarchies, etc.) that younger students can find too theoretical. Instead, younger ones often benefit from functional decomposition (breaking a task into subroutines) before moving to full OOP. We can integrate a gentle OOP introduction around age 15-16: e.g. perhaps show how a "class" can be like a record of data with attached procedures. The cross-compiler's addition of classes for  $16+$  should coincide with their math maturity (algebra) and exposure to formal logic in school, which typically is in place by that age 33. There are exceptions - some bright pre-teens can pick up OOP if taught with games or relatable metaphors - but curriculum-wise, we target OOP for the second tier. - Type systems and generics: These are subtle. A strong static type system is beneficial for catching errors (Pascal's strong typing is a plus at any age). But advanced type system features (generic templates, variant records, etc.) can confuse beginners. We'll likely keep the micro-compiler's type system simple (integers, reals, booleans, perhaps arrays/records) and only in the full compiler expose things like generics or maybe pointers. Research suggests novices often struggle with abstract types or too many data structure options - it's better to stick to a few basic ones until they understand the concepts. - Manual memory management vs. garbage collection: It's generally agreed that manual memory management (maloc/free or pointers to heap) is beyond the comfort of most K-12 students. Pascal's new dispose for pointers might be entirely omitted or hidden in the micro-compiler tier (or allowed only in very controlled scenarios) to prevent confusion and crashes. At the full compiler stage (especially for university level), discussing memory, pointers, and low-level details becomes appropriate as they tie into systems programming and architecture courses. The timing of introducing pointers could be late high school for advanced students or early university; by then, students better grasp indirection and its uses/dangers.

Educational psychology also emphasizes the importance of contextual and project-based learning for retention. The two-tier model allows us to tailor projects to the student's level: Younger students can do small games, simple math simulations, or hardware tinkering (e.g. blinking an LED, reading a sensor) on the Z80 device – these projects make abstract computing concepts tangible. Older students, using the crosscompiler, can tackle larger projects like developing a full game, an operating system kernel for the Zeal Computer, or a compiler for a subset of Pascal themselves – sophisticated tasks that require the advanced language features. By aligning project complexity with cognitive readiness, we keep students in the “zone of proximal development” – challenged enough to learn, but not so overwhelmed that they give up.

In conclusion, pedagogically SuperPascal's dual approach is well-founded: start with a safe, simple subset to build core understanding and confidence, then progressively introduce complexity as learners are ready. Foundational languages for children avoid complexity by design – not due to lack of ambition, but to ensure kids focus on key concepts without cognitive overload 29. Our strategy resonates with established frameworks (spiral curriculum, scaffolding, gradual release of responsibility) and cognitive research. It also aligns with successful modern approaches like Code.org's progression (block coding  $\rightarrow$  simple text coding  $\rightarrow$  full programming) and the UK curriculum (Scratch in primary school  $\rightarrow$  Python or similar by secondary). By the time students reach the full compiler and its richer Pascal dialect, they will have the mental tools to

absorb advanced concepts meaningfully, having been methodically prepared by years of scaffolded learning.

# Technical Viability on Z80 and Host Platforms

Designing a two-tier compiler system spanning an 8-bit Z80 and a modern host raises critical technical questions. Here we assess why the Z80 cannot host a "full-fat" modern compiler, how a trimmed one-pass compiler can succeed on the Z80, and how the host-based cross-compiler will work in tandem.

Why not put a full modern compiler on the Z80? The limitations of the Z80 (and similar 8-bit CPUs like 6502) are severe by today's standards. A Z80 typically addresses only 64KB of memory (even with bank-switching tricks, contiguous memory is limited). It runs at a few MHz clock speed (the original Z80 was  $\sim$ 4 MHz; even modern derivatives are under 50 MHz in legacy mode). Memory is the number one constraint: A modern compiler (think of something like Free Pascal or GCC) uses many megabytes of memory to store abstract syntax trees, perform optimizations, manage symbol tables, etc. That's utterly impossible on 64KB - which must also hold the compiler's own code and any OS it runs under. Historical compilers on Z80 (under CP/M or standalone) were forced to use single-pass techniques and often offloaded to disk storage due to RAM limits  $^{42}$ $^{43}$ . As an example, even a fairly simple C compiler on CP/M would write out intermediate files to floppy disk because it couldn't hold the whole program in memory  $^{42}$ . Disk I/O on 8-bit systems was painfully slow (multiple seconds to read/write a few KB)  $^{42}$ , which made multi-pass compilers very sluggish. Turbo Pascal's brilliance was avoiding intermediate files altogether by compiling in one go directly to output, making it orders of magnitude faster in practice  $^{17}$ $^{15}$ . If we attempted a full modern Pascal compiler on the Z80 (with features like OOP, generics, heavy optimization passes), it would either crash from lack of memory or thrash storage - likely taking minutes or hours to compile a single program, which is unacceptable in a classroom setting. The CPU speed is another factor: advanced compilation algorithms (data flow analysis, register allocation, etc.) would be excruciatingly slow on a few MHz. To contextualize, an optimizing C++ compiler can tax a 3 GHz PC for many seconds or minutes on large code; scaling that down to 4 MHz ( $\sim$ 0.004 GHz) is inconceivable. The Z80 also lacks hardware multiply/divide and has a modest register set, so even processing big integers or managing pointers in the compiler code would be slow. The addressing modes (16-bit pointers, no direct memory beyond 64K segment) mean even managing large code or symbol tables becomes cumbersome (hence why many Z80 programs use overlays or bank-switching). In short, a "full fat" compiler would not fit and not run efficiently on the Z80. This is why we split the problem: the micro-compiler on Z80 will intentionally be limited in scope and sophistication, and all heavy-duty compilation tasks are delegated to a cross-compiler running on a powerful host (PC).

Feasibility of a one-pass Pascal subset compiler on Z80: History strongly indicates this is feasible. Turbo Pascal 3.0 is proof-of-concept that a one-pass native code Pascal compiler can run in  $\sim 32\mathrm{KB}$  of memory (Turbo Pascal's footprint was small enough to run on 64KB CP/M systems, including its editor and runtime). Modern reimplementations like PASTA/80 confirm that even today one can fit a robust subset of Pascal for Z80  $^{19}$ $^{45}$ . The key is to design the language subset to avoid things that absolutely require multi-pass analysis. Pascal in its classic form was designed with one-pass compilation in mind  $^{14}$ . For example, functions must be declared before use (so no need for a full forward-declaration resolution pass beyond maybe one lookahead for procedure calls), and the language does not require backpatching except maybe for some goto. Our SuperPascal micro-compiler can leverage this by adopting a subset akin to Turbo Pascal 3 or ISO Pascal, excluding features that complicate one-pass compilation. Likely, the micro-compiler will not support full separate compilation of units or linking of multiple modules (every program is a single

source file or a few includes), not support advanced optimizations (just straightforward codegen with maybe a tiny peephole optimizer), and not include complex features like generics, run-time type info, or extensive reflection. By constraining to the essentials, we keep the compiler simple. A one-pass compiler operates roughly as follows: it reads a token, parses, and immediately emits corresponding machine code or reserves space, etc., then moves on. This way it doesn't need to keep a full parse tree in memory 19 20 . It will maintain a symbol table for the current block and perhaps one for global symbols, but not much more. Any error checking is done on the fly. This yields fast compilation and low memory usage. The tradeoff is that the generated code might not be globally optimized - but that's fine for our purposes. As one expert noted, such compilers won't "compete with LLVM" on optimization and they don't try to 19 46 . Instead, they produce decent, correct code quickly. This is perfect for a learning environment on slow hardware. In fact, on a 4 MHz Z80, a one-pass compiler can compile a simple program in a matter of seconds (Turbo Pascal compiled ~2000 lines per second on a 4.77 MHz PC 15 , a Z80 might be slower, but still likely within a student's patience). We can also leverage the fact that the Zeal Computer's FPGA might allow some custom hardware acceleration or at least faster clock speeds than classic 4 MHz - perhaps our Z80 core could run at 10 MHz or more, further improving compile times. Additionally, since the environment is offline and self-contained, even a moderately slow compile is acceptable if the students' programs are small. For younger kids, programs will typically be on the order of tens or a couple hundred lines, which a one-pass compiler can handle swiftly.

One challenge is that even a one-pass compiler must fit with the program and any runtime in memory. The Zeal Computer likely doesn't have a multitasking OS, so we can probably use all memory for the compiler when it runs, then chain-load the compiled program. We might adopt the model of early microcomputers: the compiler is an interactive environment itself. Possibly, the micro-compiler could double as an IDE where the student types code, then invokes "run" which triggers compile+execute in one go, similar to Turbo Pascal's IDE or BASIC's RUN command. This was an advantage noted with Turbo Pascal: an integrated editor+compiler avoided slow disk swaps 17. On the Zeal, without fast disk, integrating them is wise. We could store the source in RAM, compile to RAM, then jump to the user program, all in memory. With  $\sim 64\mathrm{KB}$  total, this is tight but doable if the programs are small and the compiler is trimmed. Perhaps the Zeal has bank-switched memory or ROM that can hold parts of the compiler - we can design around the hardware we have (since it's an FPGA system, maybe it has more than 64KB physically, but the Z80 can only see 64KB at a time). Many CP/M compilers solved space issues by overlaying code: e.g., reading one pass from disk, then another. We might not want disk usage for speed reasons, but if the machine has, say, an SD card or solid-state storage, we could swap compiler phases in and out if absolutely necessary. However, it's preferable to keep it one-phase to avoid that.

It's important to clarify what exactly the micro-compiler supports. Based on goals for ages 10-15, it will include: basic types (integers, booleans, chars, perhaps real numbers if we have a software FP library), variables and arithmetic, control structures (if, case, loops), procedures/functions (probably without recursion unless we allocate a software stack - recursion might be optional given limited stack space, but we could allow it with caution), simple I/O (reading keyboard, printing to screen, perhaps drawing using provided routines), and possibly small-scale dynamic memory (e.g., allow new on a pointer for an advanced 15-year-old project, but this could be omitted or simplified). It might also allow inline assembly for those learning low-level, just as BBC BASIC did - this can be a reward for advanced learners without complicating the high-level language semantics (the compiler can just insert bytes). By omitting units/ modules, each program stands alone, avoiding the complexity of linking. All these restrictions are acceptable in a first-tier educational setting. They prevent fragmentation because the full compiler will

support all these features and more; any code written for the micro-compiler is essentially a subset of what the full compiler accepts (more on language unity later).

Architecture of the host-based cross-compiler (full compiler): The second tier is a Rust-based cross-compiler running on a PC or server, targeting the Z80 (and possibly other 8-bit/16-bit targets like 65C816 as mentioned). This compiler will generate Z80 machine code or assembly that can then be loaded onto the Zeal Computer for execution. Essentially, it acts like a modern development toolchain: think of how one might cross-compile C code on a PC to run on an Arduino's AVR microcontroller. The advantages are clear: the host machine has virtually unlimited CPU and memory compared to the Z80, so we can afford full semantic analysis, optimization, and advanced language features. Students aged  $16+$  (or university students) will typically have access to a PC lab or at least a more powerful machine where this cross-compiler can run. The cross-compiler will likely produce either a binary program or a HEX file that can be transferred to the Zeal Computer (via serial, SD card, etc.). We might even provide an emulator of the Zeal on the PC so students can test quickly before deploying to the actual hardware - this hybrid workflow is common and actually beneficial (fast turnaround on PC, then final test on device).

The host compiler's architecture could re-use components of an existing compiler (perhaps piggyback on an existing Pascal front-end or LLVM backend), but since it's noted to be Rust-based and custom, it suggests we'll implement it largely from scratch (or using Rust for safety and performance). It will support the full SuperPascal language: including units (modular compilation), classes and inheritance, virtual methods and dynamic dispatch, exceptions, etc. These features imply a much more complex compilation process: - Separate compilation/units: The compiler will need to output object code or assembly for units and link them together. We might integrate with an assembler/linker for Z80 (for example, PASTA/80 uses an external Z80 assembler, sjasmplus 47 48). We could either produce raw machine code or assembly that then goes through an assembler. Using an existing Z80 assembler as a backend is sensible to avoid writing our own code generator from scratch - our compiler could output textual assembly which is then assembled. Alternatively, the compiler could directly emit binary (like Turbo Pascal did) since writing a Z80 code emitter is not too hard. But integrating with a known tool (like the open-source z88dk or sjasmplus) can speed up development. - Classes/OOP: Implementing Object-Oriented features on Z80 is doable but must be carefully designed. We have to implement vtables (virtual method tables) in a 16-bit address space, handle constructor/destructor calls, etc. The cross-compiler can handle the heavy lifting of laying out class structures in memory and generating appropriate calls. The resulting programs on the Z80 might be slower due to indirection for method calls, but that's acceptable for educational use. We should ensure that the micro-compiler (which won't support classes) and cross-compiler still share syntax for other constructs so that transitioning code from one to the other is straightforward. - Exception handling: This is advanced - likely implemented via setjmp/longjmp style or stack unwinding. On an 8-bit system with no built-in OS, true exception handling is complex. We may restrict exceptions to the cross-compiled programs (the microcompiler likely won't have them at all). The cross-compiler's runtime library on the Z80 might include an error/exception handler that can unwind the stack. But note, exception support will consume memory (for stack unwinding data, etc.) and CPU. It's something we'd only enable or use in more complex student projects. An alternative is to implement a simpler "assertion" or error-check mechanism in the microcompiler and reserve real exception semantics (try/catch) for cross-compiled programs when performance allows. - Optimizations: The cross-compiler can implement various optimizations (common subexpression elimination, loop unrolling, etc.), but one must question how much is beneficial given the Z80's constraints. Highly optimized code is nice (especially to get decent performance out of 4 MHz), but a very optimized code generator may produce code harder for students to understand if they study assembly output (which they might in advanced courses). We could consider providing different optimization levels. Perhaps in an

educational context, readability and consistency of generated code is more important than squeezing every cycle. For example, it might be better that the code generator produces a straightforward translation that students can trace (if we're teaching compilers or assembly), instead of an aggressively optimized one. However, given one of the selling points of Pascal historically was that it could generate fast executables even on slow hardware 12 , we do want reasonably efficient code so that complex student projects (like games) run smoothly on the Zeal Computer. We can strike a balance by implementing a moderate set of optimizations in the cross-compiler. Peephole optimizations (small pattern replacements in generated assembly) are relatively simple and yield good results on Z80 - PASTA/80, for example, has a peephole optimizer and a dependency analyzer to remove unused functions 49 50 . We can do similarly. Wholeprogram optimizations aren't as crucial for our scale. - ABI details: The full compiler will define an Application Binary Interface (ABI) for the Z80 platform. This includes how function calls work (register vs stack parameter passing, who saves registers, etc.), how the stack is managed, how global and static data are laid out, and how units link together. We must design this ABI carefully to allow interoperability: for instance, if a library unit is compiled with the cross-compiler, the micro-compiled programs could still call routines from it (assuming they stick to subset features). This implies we'll likely use a consistent calling convention across both compilers. The micro-compiler might use a very simple calling convention (e.g., all parameters on stack, or a fixed set of registers for parameters) to keep code generation easy. The crosscompiler can use the same for compatibility or might support multiple conventions for optimization. Given educational focus, having a single straightforward calling convention (like Pascal's classic stack frames with BP registers, etc.) is beneficial, and indeed examining that could be part of the advanced curriculum (students could inspect stack frames and see how parameters are passed). Historically, different Pascal compilers had their own ABIs, but since we control both compilers, we can ensure they align on at least a common subset ABI. - Interoperability without fragmentation: A critical design goal is that despite one compiler being limited and the other advanced, they produce programs that can run on the same platform and call into the same runtime libraries. We must avoid creating two separate worlds that never meet. One approach is to structure the language such that any program that conforms to the subset (Tier-1) can also be compiled by the full compiler with identical behavior. That means the full compiler must support all the subset's semantics (which is likely, since the subset is just a restriction of the full Pascal). For example, if the subset doesn't support units, a student's single-file program compiled on-device should also compile with the cross-compiler (perhaps we supply a trivial unit or module wrapper for it). Conversely, if an advanced student writes code using classes and tries to run it on the micro-compiler, it will not compile - but that's expected and by design. This is similar to how MicroPython vs CPython works: MicroPython is a subset of Python 3, so any MicroPython code usually runs on CPython, but not vice versa 51 . We might explicitly document which features are subset-only. To maintain long-term compatibility, we should avoid making gratuitous changes to the language between tiers. Ideally, the syntax and keywords are the same, only the availability of certain features changes. For instance, the keyword unit might be reserved in the micro-compiler (to avoid confusion) but produce an error if used; students will learn about it later in the cross-compiler context. This prevents fragmentation of "dialects." The danger of fragmentation is illustrated by, say, Python 2 vs Python 3 or other language splits - it can confuse users and reduce synergy. We intend SuperPascal to be one language with two levels of enforcement.

Two compilers, one language environment: How do we ensure they interoperate? One key aspect is the runtime library and system calls. The Zeal Computer will have certain low-level routines (for graphics, sound, file I/O if any, etc.). We should implement these routines (or BIOS calls) in such a way that both compilers' outputs can use them. Likely we'll provide a standard library (perhaps akin to Turbo Pascal's CRT unit, etc.) that the cross-compiler can link and the micro-compiler can call. The micro-compiler might even include some of these routines as built-ins or in a ROM. To avoid duplication, it could make sense for the

micro-compiler's runtime to actually be compiled by the cross-compiler and stored in the system (for instance, maybe the graphics library is too complex to write in Z80 assembly by hand; instead, we implement it in Pascal with advanced features, cross-compile it, and include the binary in the ROM). This approach leverages the cross-compiler to produce optimized library code that the micro environment can use. However, care is needed to ensure the subset compiler "understands" the interfaces. We may treat certain complex routines as black boxes that the micro-compiler just knows how to call (similar to how BASIC had ROM routines). This cooperation ensures we don't fragment libraries - we have one set of core libraries for SuperPascal, used regardless of compiler, although possibly with different internal implementations or performance profiles.

Z80 as a target for modern compiled code: It's worth noting that while Z80 is old, it is still serviceable for educational projects. As one Hacker News commenter noted, "people absolutely still design around [Z80]... there's even an updated eZ80 still made" 52 . The Z80 has a rich software ecosystem and known instruction set, so our cross-compiler isn't reinventing the wheel from scratch. We can use existing assemblers, linkers, and even draw on existing Z80 compiler backends (the Free Pascal community, for example, has worked on a Z80 cross-compiler). The main technical risk is ensuring that the advanced features can execute within the limited RAM and speed. A complex program compiled with classes and such may simply be too slow or too big for 8-bit hardware. We must set expectations: students using the advanced compiler to make large programs must understand the target's limits. For instance, an elaborate software with many classes and layers of abstraction might run slowly on 4 MHz - part of the learning could be to appreciate efficiency and possibly use profiling or optimization (this itself is educational, bridging into topics of algorithmic efficiency and low-level performance tuning). If extremely needed, the FPGA could allow a faster mode (some educational devices allow turbo speeds for later use). But likely, careful coding and the small scale of student projects will be fine.

In summary, the two-tier toolchain is technically viable if we partition responsibilities cleverly: - The microcompiler: single-pass, fits in memory, minimal features, immediate codegen. It prioritizes simplicity and reliability over performance of generated code (though generated code should be decent enough). It may produce slightly larger or slower code than a multi-pass compiler, but given the typical program sizes, that's acceptable. (One modern observation: PASTA/80 mentioned its generated binaries were larger than original Turbo Pascal's, partly because the runtime was compiled from Pascal rather than hand-tuned assembly, but improvements were planned 53. We should be mindful of code size, but since our environment is educational, an extra kilobyte or two for clarity or safety is fine.) - The full compiler: multi-pass (likely), runs on PC, outputs efficient Z80 machine code. It can handle bigger programs and incorporate modern language conveniences. We will treat it as the "reference implementation" of SuperPascal, whereas the microcompiler is a pedagogical subset implementation.

Finally, a note on maintenance: Having two compilers means double the implementation effort. To ease this, we can reuse code or at least logic between them. Perhaps the micro-compiler can be written in a subset of Pascal that is also used by the cross-compiler (for instance, PASTA/80's compiler is written in Pascal and compiled by Free Pascal 24). We might write the micro-compiler in the SuperPascal language itself, and compile it with the cross-compiler – this is a form of bootstrapping. Alternatively, we write it in C or assembly for tight control. But a nice goal would be that advanced students could even read the source code of their micro-compiler (since it's small) to learn about compilers – making the compiler itself an educational artifact. The cross-compiler being in Rust is fine (it ensures performance and memory safety on PC), but perhaps parts of it (like the front-end grammar) could be shared or at least kept consistent with the

micro-compiler's front-end. If we base both on the same language spec (which we will), keeping them aligned is mostly a matter of discipline and thorough testing.

In essence, the technical strategy is: use the right tool for the right environment. The Z80 gets a lean, mean compiler that respects its limits, and the PC does the heavy lifting for anything beyond that. This division of labor is exactly why cross-Compilation is common in embedded systems today (no one would compile Android apps on the phone itself – they cross-compile on a PC for a reason). We adapt that paradigm for education, ensuring students see a continuous experience (the language feels the same) even though under the hood, two very different compilers are working.

# Safety and Cultural Context for Offline Computing (Bulgaria and Beyond)

In proposing an offline-first educational platform, we must consider the broader social, cultural, and safety context - especially given the Bulgarian setting mentioned. There is a growing concern among parents and educators worldwide about young students having unrestricted access to the internet and general-purpose computers. Bulgaria is currently exemplifying this concern: the Ministry of Education (BG) is preparing policies to ban "full-fat" operating systems with browsers in classrooms for students under 17, due to parental pressure about internet exposure. In fact, from January 2026, Bulgarian schools are set to ban mobile phones entirely during school hours  $^{54}$ , citing the "negative impact of screen time on children's health, education, and personal development"  $^{55}$ . The Education Minister noted that excessive device use (especially for non-educational purposes) leads to missed learning and developmental opportunities  $^{56}$ . This push extends to exploring social media restrictions for under-15s due to documented negative effects  $^{57}$ . These developments underline a strong desire for controlled, intentional use of technology in schools - exactly the philosophy of the SuperPascal ecosystem.

Offline computing is essential for younger learners for several reasons: - Distraction-free learning: When devices have internet and multi-purpose OS capabilities, the temptation for distraction is high. A locked-down educational computer like the Zeal (which presumably boots straight into a programming environment, no social media, no YouTube) keeps students focused on the task of learning programming. Teachers in many countries struggle with students alt-tabbing to games or websites; an appliance-like microcomputer removes that issue by design. - Safety from harmful content: The internet, while immensely valuable, harbors inappropriate or dangerous content for children. By restricting or eliminating internet access at school, we remove the risk of students accidentally (or intentionally) encountering explicit content, cyberbullying on social media, or other harms. Bulgaria's initiative to ban phones aligns with protecting children from such content during school hours  $^{56}$ . Our system would take that further by having no browser or external connectivity at all on the under-16 machines. Parents will appreciate that their kids can't wander into the digital wild west unsupervised. - Avoiding dependency on AI and search for problem-solving: Another modern risk is the over-reliance on AI tools (like code generators or even search engines) that can short-circuit the learning process. If a student can just Google an answer or have an AI write a program, they may not develop their own problem-solving skills. Psychology experts warn: "If we let AI handle too much of the learning process, we risk raising kids who are good with tech but need help thinking on their own"  $^{58}$ . In a critical learning phase, children need to struggle through problems to build cognitive skills. An offline system forces them to think and create code themselves, rather than ask Google or ChatGPT for help at every step. This controlled environment thus preserves the pedagogical integrity of assignments. - Mitigating early exposure to AI-driven content: There have been alarming instances of AI

systems giving dangerous advice to children. For example, Amazon's Alexa once advised a child to plug a coin into an electrical outlet (a clearly dangerous act), and Snapchat's AI chatbot gave inappropriate suggestions to a minor  $^{59}$ . These incidents highlight that unmonitored AI or internet usage can pose real safety hazards. By keeping the computing environment offline and curated, we eliminate those unpredictable AI interactions. Any educational content provided is vetted by educators, not dynamically generated by an algorithm with unknown reliability. - Data privacy and security: An offline device does not collect or transmit student data over networks. In an era of data breaches and privacy concerns, this is reassuring. Especially when dealing with minors, keeping their data local (or on school servers only) is often a legal requirement (e.g. COPPA in the US, GDPR in EU for minors). Our ecosystem inherently respects privacy by design - there's no pipeline to send student code or performance data to external servers unless explicitly allowed for certain features. - Adaptation to local constraints: Beyond intentional restrictions, offline-first addresses inequalities in access. Globally, two-thirds of school-age children (1.3 billion kids) have no internet access at home  $^{60}$ . Even within countries like Bulgaria, rural areas or lower-income families may have limited connectivity. If our educational program relied on cloud services or online tools, many students could be left behind. An offline system ensures every student, regardless of home internet, can practice and learn (especially if they can take the device home or have sufficient time in school labs). This can help bridge the digital divide in a way that a purely online approach cannot. The UNICEF report called lack of connectivity a "digital canyon" that isolates children and robs them of opportunities  $^{61}$  - ironically, our approach is to teach computing fundamentals in a way that doesn't need connectivity, thus empowering even those on the wrong side of the digital divide with core skills. It's a different solution to the same equity problem: rather than immediately giving all kids internet, which is a massive infrastructural challenge, we give them computing knowledge that prepares them to leverage the internet effectively later on. - Cultural preservation and focus: In some regional contexts, there's also a cultural angle - concerns that kids exposed to global internet content too early might lose interest in local culture or language. An offline computing curriculum can be tailored to local language (e.g., the SuperPascal environment and documentation can be in Bulgarian for younger kids, gradually introducing English technical terms at older ages). It keeps the learning culturally relevant and controlled by local curriculum standards, rather than the random mix of internet content.

Risks of AI-driven, internet-based programming environments too early: Many ed-tech products now incorporate AI tutors or code auto-completion (like GitHub Copilot). While these can assist learning for motivated older students, for children they may do more harm than good if introduced too soon. For example, a student might rely on auto-complete to write code without understanding it, leading to a false sense of competency and shallow knowledge. Moreover, AI can sometimes produce incorrect code or explanations that a novice won't be able to critique. This could reinforce misconceptions. There's also the phenomenon of "cognitive offloading" – if kids know an AI can solve something, they may not even attempt the problem themselves, missing out on the learning that comes from trial and error. Early programming education should emphasize problem-solving, persistence, and creativity, not just finding the right answer quickly. By delaying the introduction of AI tools until students have a solid foundation, we ensure they have the skills to use those tools critically when the time comes (likely in advanced courses or in higher education). Additionally, AI chatbots have been known to sometimes output inappropriate content or exhibit biases. Children are generally not equipped to handle or filter such output, which again points to keeping their environments deterministic and educator-curated in the early years 62 .

Benefits of controlled environments: A controlled computing environment like the Zeal/SuperPascal ecosystem has multiple educational benefits: - It creates a sense of mastery and ownership. Students can understand the whole machine (it's simple enough) and the software stack. This demystifies technology.

Many technologists naughtlgically recall how using 8-bit micros gave them a deep understanding of how computers work (since one could actually comprehend the entire system). In contrast, today's PCs and OSes are black boxes to most users. By giving young learners a simpler system, we empower them to truly learn by doing, even experimenting fearlessly (because they can't break the internet or access forbidden things). - It can foster creativity through limitations. Ironically, constraints often spur creativity. Knowing that they have only the tools provided, students often invent clever solutions or games within those confines. This can cultivate ingenuity and resilience. In an open internet scenario, a student might just download a game or copy code; on a closed system, if they want a game, they might try to make it themselves. The culture that grows around such systems (like the vibrant communities that grew around the BBC Micro, Spectrum, etc.) often leads to peer learning and collaboration in a safe context. - Community and sharing: Since all students have the same machine and language, they form a community of practice. They can share programs via safe means (perhaps exchanging printouts or via teacher-approved file transfers) rather than, say, trading USB sticks full of random software. This can be monitored and guided by teachers, ensuring that what they share is appropriate. The nostalgia and identity around a platform (for example, the British generation that learned on BBC Micros still share that bond) is not trivial - it can motivate students to dig deeper, to join extracurricular clubs, etc. We could see coding clubs or competitions emerge around SuperPascal (imagine a science fair where 15-year-olds compete to create the best game on the Zeal Computer - a focused, friendly competition that doesn't involve internet connectivity). - Alignment with policy: If Bulgaria is indeed moving toward restricting general-purpose computers for under-17, a platform like ours could become the officially sanctioned solution that satisfies all stakeholders. Parents are reassured (no internet means less worry about online predators or distractions), teachers are relieved (a consistent platform easier to manage than mixed BYOD devices), and policymakers can claim they are innovating in education while protecting children. It would be a showcase example of "protective tech" - technology designed to be developmentally appropriate.

Of course, we must be cognizant of potential downsides or criticisms of an offline approach and address them. One concern: Will students fall behind in digital literacy or internet skills? While focusing on offline coding, are we neglecting the need to teach kids how to use the internet safely and effectively? The strategy should be not to shun the internet indefinitely, but to delay and structure its introduction. Perhaps up to grade 10 or so, students work primarily offline to build strong foundations. Then in later high school (16-18), alongside the introduction of the full compiler, we also introduce more open computing - controlled web research, collaborative coding platforms, etc., as a graduation to the wider world once they have maturity. This parallels how some curricula handle social media: forbid it or simulate it in lower grades, then gradually allow it with guidance in later grades. Additionally, digital literacy (typing, basic computer use) can still be taught on offline systems. Just because it's offline doesn't mean it lacks applications: one could have an encyclopedia on disk, or a local forum/BBS style discussion that is within a school's intranet. Those kinds of controlled digital experiences can impart skills without the full internet exposure.

Another possible critique: Isn't this approach somewhat retro or limiting compared to modern CS education? It is intentionally retro in some ways, but the point is to re-introduce depth over breadth. Modern kids are great at tapping and swiping, but often lack understanding of what goes on under the hood. Our approach may feel "old-school" – reminiscent of the 1980s – yet many in the tech industry argue that the 1980s generation, who often started on 8-bit micros, developed stronger algorithmic thinking early on. By bringing a modernized version of that experience (with Pascal instead of BASIC), we hope to cultivate a generation that truly understands computing, not just how to call APIs. There is a cultural element too: in Bulgaria and other Eastern European countries, there is a legacy of strong informatics education (Bulgaria had the Pravetz computers in the 80s, produced many skilled programmers). Our project can tap into

national pride in that history – a modern Bulgarian educational computer is a compelling narrative. It's not just about nostalgia; it's about self-reliance and building technical talent at home without depending on Big Tech solutions.

Finally, an offline environment can be seen as a sandbox for innovation. Students can experiment with writing viruses (benign ones) or hacking the system in a safe way (since it's not connected, any "harm" is local and part of learning security). They can create AI algorithms and test them on small data sets offline, which teaches resourcefulness (training an AI model in 64KB is a neat challenge!). In other words, constraints can spur learning of efficiency and clever techniques that bloated online tools might never necessitate.

In conclusion, the safety and cultural context strongly favor an offline-first, controlled computing environment for K-10 education, especially in places like Bulgaria where there is active effort to shield children from the open internet until they are older. SuperPascal and the Zeal Computer provide exactly what is needed: a computer that is empowering but not dangerous, educational but not overwhelming. It aligns with parental expectations and emerging policies. By keeping young learners unplugged yet deeply engaged, we create a healthier, focused educational atmosphere. We must, however, plan to gradually integrate these students into the online, connected world as they approach graduation – equipped with a solid foundation and a critical mindset that will help them navigate the internet safely when the time comes.

# Educational Progression Design (Ages 10-15 to 16+ Transition)

Designing the curriculum and learning progression is pivotal to ensure that students smoothly transition from the Z80 environment to the more advanced compiler and broader computer science concepts. We outline a structured path that corresponds to cognitive and skill development stages: - Phase 1: Ages 10-12 (Introductory Level on Z80). In this stage, students are typically in late primary or early middle school. The focus is on fundamentals of programming logic and basic computer architecture concepts, taught in a highly engaging, hands-on way. Using the Z80-native SuperPascal subset, students learn: - Basic syntax and programming constructs: printing text, reading input, using variables of simple types (integer, char, boolean). At this age, even understanding what a variable "really is" can be abstract, so concrete analogies (like a variable is a box that holds a number) and immediate visuals (maybe an on-screen representation of variables) help. - Control structures: IF...THEN for decisions, and loop constructs (FOR, WHILE, or a simplified repeat-until) to understand repetition. They likely start with very concrete tasks (e.g., loop to draw a border on the screen, or to add numbers). Each concept is tied to a small project or puzzle. - Procedural decomposition: Probably by age 11-12, they can start writing simple procedures/functions in Pascal. The micro-compiler supports procedures, so we encourage breaking tasks into subroutines (e.g., a procedure to draw a square, which they then call multiple times to draw a pattern). This also introduces the idea of parameters and return values in a gentle way. - Mental model of the computer: Because this is an offline system, we have an opportunity to integrate learning of hardware basics. For example, we can show them a simplified schematic of the Zeal Computer, explaining that the Z80 CPU fetches instructions, etc. Using the actual machine, they can maybe toggle into an interactive machine code stepper (some 8-bit systems had monitors to step through assembly). It might be too early for most 10-12 year-olds to code in assembly, but understanding a bit of "under the hood" fosters curiosity and builds intuition. We can keep it simple (like showing how variables reside at memory addresses, maybe have a command to peek at memory). - Creative projects: Young students should frequently do creative, rewarding projects. For instance, writing a program to generate a simple tune (if the system supports sound), or a text-based adventure, or a basic game

(guess-the-number, etc.). These projects make them apply what they learned in a fun way and show off to peers/parents. The key is these projects remain manageable in scope (couple dozen lines of code max initially) and focus on using the constructs they just learned.

Throughout Phase 1, concepts of CPU architecture are introduced informally: e.g., explaining what the Z80's role is, what memory is (perhaps using the classic analogy of memory as a series of mailboxes each with an address). We might incorporate a bit of history too - telling them they are programming a type of computer that powered classic arcade games or early PCs can be exciting and culturally enriching. Given Bulgaria's computing heritage, we could tie in references to the Pravetz-8M (a Bulgarian Apple II clone) or other local tech history, making them feel part of a lineage of innovators.

- Phase 2: Ages 13–15 (Intermediate Level on Z80, preparation for transition). In this middle stage (corresponding roughly to middle school to early high school), students have a grasp of basics and can handle more complexity. The SuperPascal micro-compiler will now be used to teach:  
- Data structures: Introduce arrays, and maybe records (Pascal's struct-like construct). For example, at 13 they might learn to use an array to store a list of numbers and compute average, etc. At 14-15, they can handle records (structures with fields) to group related data. These concepts prepare them for thinking in terms of structured data, a stepping stone to classes later.  
- More algorithmic thinking: Cover sorting algorithms, simple search, maybe recursion for those who can handle it (the micro-compiler could allow recursion if stack-checking is in place 63). We can actually demonstrate recursion visually (like via the call stack display, if we have a debugging tool, or by simple recursive drawings). However, recursion is conceptually advanced and not all students will internalize it at 14; it could be treated as an enrichment topic for those ready, while others can get by with loops.  
- Advanced control flow: Possibly introduce the concept of a state machine or more complex program structures. Error handling at this stage might still be primitive (maybe using return codes or flags, since exceptions are not in the subset), but we can start encouraging robust programming practices (like checking inputs, handling edge cases).  
- Introduction to assembly and CPU internals: By 15, interested students might delve a bit into Z80 assembly language. We could have a module or elective content where they write a very small routine in assembly and link it (the micro-compiler supports inline assembly  $^{64}$ , so they could embed a few instructions, perhaps to optimize something or to directly manipulate hardware registers). This cements their understanding of how high-level code maps to low-level operations. Even those who don't write much assembly should be taught to read simple assembly snippets and correlate them with Pascal constructs. For instance, show them how a loop in Pascal corresponds to a jump and a compare in assembly – this demystifies "magic" and fosters an appreciation for efficiency.  
- Basic computer architecture lessons: We expand on architecture knowledge: perhaps cover the concept of a CPU instruction set, registers, what the ALU is, etc. The 65C816 or 6502 might be introduced conceptually as comparison (the prompt mentions 65C816 – maybe the Zeal Computer or a variant could have a 65C816 too, or maybe a future expansion). We can discuss how the Z80 differs from 6502, not in heavy detail, but to illustrate architectural variety and what it means to target a different CPU. This is in preparation for broader understanding in Phase 3 and beyond.  
- Project-based learning: In this phase, students can undertake more ambitious projects on the Z80. For instance, writing a simple game like Snake or Tetris clone (text-based or simple graphics) – something that might be 200-300 lines of code, pushing the micro-compiler to its comfortable limit. They might also do hardware-oriented projects, since presumably the Zeal Computer could interface

with sensors or robotics. A project where they program a small robot or a device (with the Z80 controlling it) could integrate programming with physics/math, making learning interdisciplinary. These projects should be designed to solidify understanding of loops, conditionals, and data handling. Students will start to hit the limits of the micro-compiler (for example, they might want to organize code across multiple files or crave some feature like dynamic memory or a larger library). This sense of hitting a ceiling is intentional – it motivates them for the next step.

By the end of Phase 2 (around age 15-16), students should be fluent in procedural programming, comfortable with the concept of a machine, and aware that more powerful tools exist beyond their current one. This is an excellent point to introduce some theory: perhaps a light introduction to what compilers do ("We've been using a simple compiler; did you know bigger compilers do multiple passes, optimize code, etc.?"). We can also foreshadow concepts like object-oriented design by relating it to things they know ("Records group data, what if we could also group behaviors? That's what classes will allow").

- Phase 3: Ages 16–18 (Advanced Level on Cross-Compiler, pre-University). This corresponds to high school juniors and seniors, who often are taking specialized CS courses or preparing for university entrance. At this stage, we transition students to the full SuperPascal compiler running on a host PC (while still targeting the Zeal Computer for execution, at least initially). Key aspects of this phase:  
- Object-Oriented Programming: We introduce classes, objects, inheritance, and polymorphism. This could be done by building on their prior knowledge of records and procedures: a class can be seen as a record (fields) plus procedures (methods) that know how to operate on that record. We show how inheritance allows a hierarchy (e.g., maybe make a simple game where you have a base class Sprite and subclasses Player, Enemy with specialized behaviors). Virtual methods can be taught by example (perhaps an array of Sprite pointers that actually point to different subclass objects - demonstrating dynamic dispatch). We must be careful to not let the excitement of OOP overshadow fundamental considerations - so we also teach when to use it and when simpler structures suffice.  
- Advanced type system and generics: If SuperPascal supports generics or templates, this is the time to cover them. Perhaps writing a generic list container or using one from a provided library. They can appreciate the convenience generics provide after having done without them (for example, they might have written separate routines for arrays of int vs arrays of char in earlier years; now see how a generic could unify that).  
- Memory management: We unveil pointers and dynamic allocation in a deeper way. Students should learn how the heap and stack work, perhaps by examining the assembly output of the crosscompiler or using debugging tools. For instance, allocate objects dynamically and discuss how pointers work. The concept of manual memory management vs. garbage collection can be discussed (maybe SuperPascal uses manual Dispose, which is very much like C's free). This segues into issues of memory safety and why languages like Rust (which our compiler is written in) were created. It could be eye-opening for them to realize the cross-compiler is built in Rust, a modern safe language, whereas they are writing in Pascal which has some unsafe aspects (dangling pointers, etc.). This comparative view enriches their understanding of programming language design and the trade-offs between control and safety.  
- Exception handling: Now they can learn about try-except blocks, exception propagation, and how it compares to simpler error-handling approaches they used earlier. A good exercise is to let them implement robust file I/O routines that handle errors (like file not found, etc.) using exceptions, showing a cleaner structure than checking error codes.

- Compilation and linking in depth: Because they are now using a cross-compiler, we should teach them about the compilation process itself. This might include:

- How source code is parsed into an AST (we could show them a simplified AST dump of a program).  
- How the compiler does semantic checks (maybe demonstrate an example of a semantic error that is caught).  
- How code generation works: perhaps compile a small function and then walk them through the generated Z80 assembly. Because they know Z80 assembly from earlier phases, they can now appreciate the compiler's output and perhaps even tweak their code to affect it (e.g., inline assembly or understanding how high-level constructs map to low-level code).  
- Linking: Explain how separate units/modules are compiled into object files and then linked. We could actually have them create a multi-module project - e.g., one unit for a math library, one for the main program - to experience separate compilation. They could use the crosscompiler's features to build these and then see the final binary loaded on the Zeal.  
- Toolchain utilities: Introduce any supporting tools, like an assembler or debugger. Perhaps the cross-compiler suite includes a debugger where they can set breakpoints in their Pascal code (the cross-compiler could emit debug info that an emulator or hardware debugger uses). Learning to debug is crucial at this stage.

- Introduction to broader CS concepts: With fundamentals down, older students can handle topics like:

$\circ$  Algorithms and complexity: We can frame their earlier algorithm work in terms of Big O notation now. For example, analyze the complexity of their sorting routine. This ties into why writing efficient code matters on limited hardware.  
Operating systems and ABIs: Perhaps have a module where they learn what an operating system is. The Zeal likely doesn't have a full OS, but maybe a BIOS or monitor. We can illustrate the concept of system calls or interrupts. Ambitious students could even build a simple cooperative scheduler on the Zeal to run two tasks (learning about context switching in a simple form).  
○ IR (Intermediate Representation) and advanced compiler topics: For those heading to university CS, giving a taste of how one could compile to an intermediate code (like P-code or some three-address code) and then to machine code is valuable. Maybe a simplified demonstration: for instance, show how a high-level expression might first be translated into a stack-based bytecode before final assembly, akin to how UCSD Pascal worked or how Java works (since earlier we saw UCSD Pascal leading to Java's idea of "write once, run anywhere" via p-code and JVM 65 66).  
- Architecture beyond 8-bit: At this age, they can also learn about more advanced processors (x86, ARM). Perhaps compare the Z80 program they wrote with how it would look on x86 (point out more registers, etc.). If possible, demonstrate cross-compiling their Pascal code to a different target (maybe the Rust-based compiler could, in the future, target say an ARM microcontroller or RISC-V board). This not only keeps their interest by showing modern relevancy but also reinforces that the principles they learned are universal, even if the hardware changes.

Projects in Phase 3 become quite sophisticated: - They might do a capstone project like writing a compiler for a tiny language themselves, using the knowledge gained. For example, task them with implementing a very basic language that outputs Z80 assembly - essentially making them build a minicompiler. This is ambitious but doable as a group project or for top students and is often done in university courses; doing a scaled-down version in high school is an exceptional enrichment. - Build a more complex game or application that possibly involves the Zeal Computer networked or communicating with a PC. For instance,

an 18-year-old could make an IoT sensor with the Zeal and have a PC program visualize data - merging their knowledge of microcomputer programming and general computing. - Explore cross-platform development: modify their code to run not just on the Z80 Zeal but also on a PC (maybe via a Pascal compiler for PC). This teaches portability. If SuperPascal's full compiler can emit x86 or WebAssembly, they could try running their projects in those environments too.

The transition at  $\sim 16$  from micro-compiler to cross-compiler is a big shift akin to moving from driving a gokart to driving a car. We should support it with oriented instruction: an initial course or module specifically on "Using the new SuperPascal compiler and advanced features." In practice, we might have them continue to use the Zeal hardware as the execution target for continuity, but now their code is written in an IDE on a PC. There's a practical matter: schools would need PCs for the older students to run the Rust-based compiler and perhaps more advanced IDE (unless the cross-compiler itself has an IDE mode on PC). Many schools do have computer labs by high school, so this is feasible. - Phase 4: University Level and Beyond (Optional for our scope, but to ensure continuity). The user prompt mentions university-level CS education as a target for the full compiler as well. In designing SuperPascal, we should consider alignment with common university curricula. # Systems Design Implications for SuperPascal

Designing SuperPascal to serve both a micro-compiler and a full compiler, across a decade of student development, imposes several system design requirements. We need a language and toolchain that remain coherent between the two tiers while accommodating vastly different resource conditions. Here we address how to ensure unified syntax/semantics, long-term compatibility, toolchain components, and the overall ecosystem.

Unified Syntax and Semantics: It is crucial that SuperPascal be defined as a single language, not two disjoint dialects. The micro-compiler will implement a subset of that language specification, and the full compiler will implement (ideally) the entire spec plus possibly some extensions for expert use. To avoid confusion: - We will maintain one official Language Reference Manual for SuperPascal. Within it, we might annotate certain features as "advanced - available only in Tier-2 compiler" for clarity. For example, the manual might describe the class construct but note it requires the cross-compiler (so younger students simply skip that chapter until appropriate). - All fundamental aspects (expression syntax, control flow keywords, basic types, etc.) are identical in both tiers. For instance, if micro-Pascal has WHILE DO, the full Pascal will too (perhaps with additional variations like FOR EACH if we add that, but nothing in the subset gets removed or altered in meaning). - Data type sizes and behaviors should be consistent: If an Integer is 16-bit on the Z80 compiler, the cross-compiler should treat Integer as 16-bit as well when targeting Z80. This is important so that when students move up, their intuition about overflow or range remains valid. (We could define a type like Integer16 explicitly or just state that on the 8-bit platform, Integer = 16-bit by design.) If the full compiler were later targeting 32-bit platforms, we might introduce a larger type, but for programs intended for Z80, keeping the same definition prevents subtle bugs. In essence, the ABI from the language perspective should be uniform: e.g., calling conventions, alignment, endianness assumptions, etc., all match between compilers so that compiled modules can interoperate. - We will likely reserve keywords in the subset compiler for advanced features to avoid divergence. For instance, UNIT, CLASS, TRY, etc., could be reserved words even if the subset doesn't implement them, ensuring code written in the subset doesn't accidentally use those as identifiers and then break when compiled with full compiler. This forward-compatibility is a known tactic (e.g., some BASICs reserved certain words for future expansion). - Error and warning messages should have a similar tone or format across compilers. A student who sees a compile error in the micro-compiler should not be baffled by completely different phrasing in the cross-compiler. We can unify the style (maybe even share some code for error reporting). Consistency in messaging helps students because they don't have to learn a new "language of errors."

Long-term compatibility between micro-compiler and full compiler: We aim for a situation where code developed in early years can still run (or be built) in later years with minimal changes, to emphasize continuity. Some strategies: - Encourage clean coding practices from the start that will be upward compatible. For example, if the subset lacks modules, young students might tend to write one big file with global variables. When they transition, they might need to refactor to use units for better organization. To ease this, we might simulate some modularity in the subset level by conventions (like encouraging them to logically separate code sections, or perhaps we provide a simple include mechanism for multiple source files in micro-compiler 67). If the micro-compiler supports an %INCLUDE directive 67 for including code, that's already something the full compiler will likely support or have an equivalent. This way, if a student already learned to break their program into included files, moving to units in the full compiler is conceptually similar. - Ensure the runtime libraries are consistent. If a student uses WriteLn or Random() in micro-Pascal, those should exist and behave the same in full Pascal (perhaps with identical

parameter types and effects) 22 68. The micro-compiler might implement them differently (maybe simpler), but from the language perspective, they're the same. We should actually strive to have a single source for core library routines and just compile it with appropriate compiler. For instance, the definition of WriteLn (in terms of functionality and signature) is one; on Z80 it might be implemented in assembly/ROM, on PC the cross-compiler's library might call OS routines or the Zeal's emulator. - Provide a way to integrate code between tiers if needed. A possible scenario: a 15-year-old might write a library in microPascal for something (like controlling a robot) and later as a 17-year-old, they're writing a more complex program in full Pascal but want to reuse that library. Ideally, they could just recompile that library with the full compiler to produce a unit or object file to link. This should work if we have not diverged. If some minor adjustments are needed (like adding unit headers), those should be straightforward. Conversely, one could consider if code from the full compiler could somehow be downgraded to run in the micro environment - generally no for advanced features, but simple code yes. Perhaps we could have the cross-compiler support an option to enforce subset compatibility (like a mode that warns "you used a feature not available in microcompiler"). This could be useful for educators to ensure they don't accidentally introduce an advanced construct when they intend to stick to basics. - Upgrading the micro-compiler over time without breaking user code: Over a 10-20 year program, we might update the micro-compiler or hardware (maybe a new version of the Zeal with more memory, etc.). If we do, we must remain backward compatible with earlier SuperPascal code. That means if we ever add features to the subset (say we decide to allow a limited form of string type or something in micro-Pascal), it shouldn't invalidate old programs. Given the conservative nature of the subset, this is manageable - we'd likely only add things that are extensions (so old code still works unaffected, just new code can use new stuff). Essentially, treat the language spec as something that can evolve but with great care at the subset level because changes propagate through the entire pipeline of education.

Toolchain Implications (IDEs, libraries, testing): To support learning, the tooling around the compilers must be user-friendly and robust: - Integrated Development Environments (IDEs): Young students (10-12) especially benefit from a simple, fun IDE. The micro-compiler might be accessed via a text editor on the Zeal itself (if it has video output and keyboard input, possibly the case). Alternatively, maybe they write code on paper or in a simple editor on a PC and then transfer it. But an on-device IDE akin to Turbo Pascal's or even like a REPL would be great. However, writing a full IDE on Z80 might be heavy - though Turbo Pascal did it within 64KB  $^{17}$ . We can have at least a full-screen text editor with basic features (like the BBC Micro had a simple line editor, Spectrum had line editor, but we can do better with today's knowledge). The full compiler, being on PC, can be paired with a modern IDE (maybe a VS Code extension or a simple custom IDE in a language like Python or Rust with GUI). We shouldn't overwhelm novices with too many buttons, though. Possibly, as students get older, the IDE can become more sophisticated. Perhaps: - Ages 10-12: Use the Zeal machine's built-in editor/monitor. It might be rudimentary but that's part of the charm and learning (imagine a simple blue screen editor where they type code, press F5 to run, reminiscent of 80s BASIC but actually compiling Pascal in the background). - Ages 13-15: They could continue on device or maybe start using an editor on a PC that communicates with the device for convenience (especially if programs get bigger, editing on a small 8-bit system might be slow). Perhaps a cross-platform editor with serial connection to the Zeal, so they still compile on device but edit on a comfortable PC screen. This might depend on what resources schools have (if each student has a PC plus the device, or just the device). - Ages  $16+$ : They'll be on PCs using the cross-compiler. Here an IDE that supports code navigation, debugging, etc., is ideal. Possibly an existing IDE like Lazarus (for Free Pascal) could be adapted, or a plugin for VS Code could provide syntax highlighting and integration to call our compiler and flash the result to an emulator or hardware. The IDE also should support unit testing frameworks if we introduce them (maybe by this stage, we teach writing test cases to ensure program correctness - a good habit). - Runtime libraries: We touched

on making them unified. Additional thought: the runtime library likely includes not just I/O but also math libraries, perhaps graphics routines, etc. We should structure the runtime as layered: - A core set that is always loaded or statically linked (basic I/O, memory management, maybe math). - Extension libraries that can be included as needed (e.g., a graphics library, a networking library if the device has connectivity for older students, etc.). The micro-compiler might not support dynamic linking, so these are probably compile-time includes or separate compilation units. - Both compilers should use the same API for these libraries. The micro-compiler might have some limitations (for example, its TextColor function might only support 8 colors because of hardware, whereas full one might have more if used on advanced hardware; but as long as they target the Zeal, we keep it same). - Over time, if we update libraries (say add new functions), we ensure backward compatibility (maybe maintain versioned units or have the compiler ensure old code still finds what it expects). - Testing frameworks: For educational purposes, having a way to automatically test student programs is helpful (for teachers to evaluate or for students to check their work). Perhaps develop a simple unit test framework for SuperPascal, at least for the cross-compiler (since injecting a testing harness on the Z80 might be heavy). Alternatively, harness the PC to test code intended for the Zeal by running an emulator. We might include an emulator in the toolchain so that when code is compiled, it can be run in software for automated testing (this is akin to how one might test Arduino code on a sim). Indeed, a crosscompiler plus emulator environment for home use would let students practice even if they forgot their Zeal device at school, etc. It's also great for CI if the project becomes larger (like to test the compiler itself or libraries). - For the micro-compiler, testing could be as simple as providing expected input/outputs for programs since it's more batch-like. Teachers might run student programs on sample inputs on the actual hardware or an emulator. - For older students with units and modular code, unit testing frameworks that allow assertions (the PASTA/80 included an Assert mechanism) can be introduced. This not only helps them ensure their code works but also teaches good software engineering practice.

Maintaining compatibility and avoiding fragmentation: We should consider some governance for language changes. Perhaps form a "SuperPascal Language Committee" (the prompt suggests advising one). This committee's job over the years is to control extensions or modifications in a way that doesn't break existing code or split the ecosystem. They would set rules like: - No feature is added to one compiler without plan to add to the other if relevant (except where hardware truly forbids it). - Deprecations are handled gracefully and over long timelines (if, say, we decided in the future to drop something, but likely we won't need to drop anything since Pascal is stable and our use is controlled). - They might also oversee translations of the environment (ensuring the language keywords remain in English - typically Pascal keywords are English, which is good for an international skill, but maybe error messages could be localized to Bulgarian for younger kids and then switch to English later to prepare them for global context).

Toolchain integration with curriculum: We might design the toolchain features to correspond to curriculum units. For example, the micro-compiler could have a student mode vs teacher mode. In student mode for early stage, maybe some advanced options are hidden or locked. As they progress, the teacher can unlock more features (like enabling inline assembly option when appropriate). This is a bit like how some calculators have a mode for basic vs advanced. It ensures that kids don't accidentally wander into complexity before they are ready, and it simplifies the interface initially (less menu items, etc.). Similarly, the cross-compiler's IDE could start with a simplified layout and then a student can switch on "expert mode" to get full debugger, profiler, etc. This differentiation by educational stage is not common in professional tools, but since our user base evolves significantly over time, it could be useful.

Another system design aspect is performance and memory management in the compiled programs. The micro-compiled programs might be less optimized, possibly "large" by 8-bit standards (maybe a few

kilobytes for trivial things due to library overhead). We should keep an eye on that; however, given that our hardware is dedicated, we can include sufficient memory (perhaps Zeal has 128KB with bank-swapping or more) so even not-so-optimized code can run. The cross-compiler's programs might actually achieve smaller code with optimization (Turbo Pascal 3 could compile itself in  $\sim 12\mathrm{K}$ , etc.). If fragmentation arises (like microcompiled code is bigger or less efficient than cross-compiled code for same source), it's not a big deal functionally, but it's something to note. Perhaps eventually the micro-compiler could be phased out if hardware improved (just speculation: if in 10 years even a microcontroller can run the full compiler, then we wouldn't need two; but that's far off, and even then the concept of scaffolding might remain - maybe they'd always keep the notion of beginner mode vs advanced mode).

IDE and materials integration: We should ensure that the educational materials (textbooks, examples) are in sync with the toolchain. If a textbook says "type this program and press Run", the environment better have that "Run" button obvious. For advanced parts, if curriculum says "use the debugger to step through your code", the toolchain must have a functional debugger by then. Achieving a good debugger on an 8-bit target is interesting - maybe easier done via emulator on PC, or by having some monitor on the Zeal. Possibly the Zeal's FPGA could include a debugging module that communicates with the PC IDE for breakpoints - a stretch goal but very valuable for teaching (imagine being able to freeze the Z80, examine registers, etc., from the IDE - that's real insight into computer workings).

Long-term sustainability of tools: If this platform is to be used for 10-20 years, we need to consider maintenance. The compilers (especially the Rust-based one) should be open-source or at least well-documented so that if the original developers move on, others can update it (e.g., to run on new operating systems, or fix bugs). The micro-compiler might also be open, or at least its spec well-known, so that if hardware changes (say a different 8-bit CPU version is needed due to supply issues), one could adapt it. The reference to projects like Spectrum4 suggests possibly leveraging existing open hardware designs to base on - this might also ensure a community can fix hardware issues or update the FPGA code. In systems design, choosing open standards for things like communication protocols or file formats will help - for example, maybe the cross-compiler outputs Intel HEX or some common format to load on device, not a proprietary format, so that even if our specific loader is gone, others can program the device.

Finally, balance between two compilers: We must avoid that the full compiler becomes so different in capabilities that it overshadows the micro-compiler to the point of making the latter obsolete. For instance, if in a few years computers are so cheap and safe that you could give a 10-year-old a fully locked-down Linux PC with an internet filter, some might question why not just teach Python or something directly. Our argument is pedagogical (and somewhat cultural). To sustain the two-tier model, we should continuously demonstrate the micro-compiler's efficacy at teaching basics better than a high-level environment would. Perhaps gather data in pilot programs to show improved outcomes (if we see that kids taught via 8-bit have stronger grasp). This evidence will justify the continued use of the micro-compiler tier. If at some point it becomes a burden (maybe teachers find it too limiting or it slows down class progress), there might be pressure to skip it. Good design – making it as user-friendly as possible, and integrating fun elements (games, immediate feedback) – will ensure the micro-compiler remains a beloved tool, not seen as a tedious constraint.

In summary, the systems design must elegantly unify the two tiers: one language, consistent tools, shared libraries. By planning for compatibility and using the cross-compiler's power to enrich (not divide) the ecosystem, we can avoid the pitfalls of having effectively two languages. Our guiding principle is that a student "graduates" from one compiler to the other seamlessly - much like moving from a bicycle to a

motorcycle: different power, same basic balancing principles. The SuperPascal language and toolchain must act as the continuous thread through their journey.

We should leverage that by framing our project as the solution that enables computing education to continue thriving under those restrictions. Instead of seeing “no internet” as a limitation, we present “offline-first computing” as an opportunity to refocus on fundamentals. We might craft a policy brief or whitepaper for lawmakers highlighting: - How SuperPascal ecosystem aligns with child protection policies. - How it fosters local talent and possibly keeps kids away from online dangers until they're mature (and by then, they'll be more responsible users due to their deeper understanding of tech). - Possibly even national pride: a Bulgarian-developed educational platform (if indeed the development team is local or at least the initiative is local) could put the country on the map in CS education innovation. It could be exportable to other countries with similar values or needs.

Language as a safe choice for schools: Historically, schools prefer languages that are stable, well-documented, and widely used academically. Pascal has a pedigree here; it might not be "trendy" like Python, but that's okay if the rationale is explained. Actually, one could argue Pascal (especially in its modern Object Pascal form) is seeing a bit of revival interest for embedded uses (FPC supports microcontrollers, etc.). Also, by using Pascal, we avoid licensing issues (it's not proprietary like some languages or tools). Using open source tools means no recurring license fees, which ministries appreciate.

Contingency planning: If, in long term, the program needs to be updated (say moving from 8-bit to 32-bit because eventually even microcontrollers might expect 32-bit), we should design SuperPascal to be scalable. Ideally, the high-level language can be retargeted to new hardware without changing its nature. If one day the Zeal Computer Mark 2 is based on a RISC-V 32-bit CPU, SuperPascal could simply have a new backend, and students might not even notice aside from things running faster or having more memory. This adaptability is part of sustainability. It ties back to keeping the architecture of the compilers modular enough to support new targets (Rust is good for that, maybe we design an IR and codegen separated so adding a new CPU is feasible).

10-20 year pipeline outcome: If successful, we'll have: - A pipeline where a 10-year-old in 2026 who starts with SuperPascal on Zeal can, by 2036, be entering university with  $8+$  years of rigorous CS training, possibly driving innovation in the local tech sector. - A self-renewing system: those early cohorts might come back as mentors or developers for the next gen, creating a virtuous cycle. - We should track metrics: number of students taking CS, their performance in competitions or exams, eventual career uptake in STEM, etc. Show that our pipeline yields competent programmers and engineers. That's the ultimate measure to convince stakeholders to continue support.

It's also important for sustainability to have institutional anchoring. For example, get the Ministry to formally include SuperPascal as an approved language for certain courses or allow it as language choice in exams. If there's an official Olympiad or contest, ensure Pascal is allowed or even have a special category to encourage participation using what they learned. Possibly collaborate with international contests (the International Olympiad in Informatics historically allowed Pascal – in fact, many IOI contestants used Turbo Pascal up to mid-2000s). We could aim for Bulgarian students to excel in IOI again using this strong foundation (Bulgaria had IOI success in the past). That kind of high-profile outcome would cement the program's prestige and longevity.

Pitfalls to avoid, technically and curricularly: Summarizing a few as part of advice: - Don't overwhelm early learners with features (we've addressed by tiering). - Don't let advanced tier drift into completely separate path (avoid fragmentation as discussed). - Ensure the platform is not seen as a "toy" that hinders modern skills: We counter this by demonstrating transferability of skills and by introducing modern concepts (like OOP, and eventually how what they learned connects to modern languages). - Avoid making the system too closed: ironically, while offline for learning, it shouldn't be a closed ecosystem for development. Engaging open communities, making sure it doesn't rely on one person's knowledge, etc., are keys.

By taking these policy and curriculum factors into account, we set up SuperPascal and the Zeal Computer not just as an isolated project, but as a living part of the education system, capable of evolving and thriving in the long run. The aim is to convince all stakeholders that this strategy produces better outcomes and aligns with societal values, thereby deserving long-term institutional support.

# Recommendations and Best Practices for the Two-Tier Strategy

Drawing on the multidisciplinary analysis above, here are specific recommendations to strengthen the SuperPascal two-tier compiler strategy, along with pitfalls to avoid:

- Emphasize Structured Foundations Early: Use the Z80 micro-compiler to instill rigorous programming habits from the start. Teach students structured programming and problem

decomposition in a controlled environment (no internet, no GUI distractions). Historical evidence shows this yields cleaner thinking  $^{32}$ . Pitfall to avoid: Do not introduce complex paradigms (e.g., OOP) too soon – it can overwhelm and confuse young learners  $^{32}$ . Stick to basics (variables, loops, conditions) until they are second nature.

- 2. Leverage Pascal's Safety and Clarity: Highlight in teacher training and materials that Pascal's design prevents many common novice errors - e.g., type mismatches are caught at compile time, array bounds can be checked  $^{72}$ . This encourages a "safe" learning where crashes are rare. Pitfall to avoid: Avoid teaching via unstructured languages (like unmodified BASIC) which can encourage sloppy habits (spaghetti code, undeclared variables). Even if BASIC is nostralgically appealing, Pascal's structured approach is proven to produce more reliable student programs  $^{14}$ ,  $^{26}$ .  
- 3. Keep Language Consistent Across Tiers: Define SuperPascal as one language. Ensure the subset (Tier-1) is a true subset of the full language (Tier-2) with identical semantics for overlapping features. This way, code can migrate upward seamlessly  $\begin{array}{lll} 19 & 20 & \end{array}$ . For example, if FOR loops exist in both, they should behave exactly the same. Pitfall to avoid: Divergent dialects – do not let the two compilers introduce conflicting syntax or behavior. Fragmentation will confuse both students and teachers and undermine the continuity of the curriculum.  
- 4. Implement a Graduated Toolchain Interface: Design the development environment to grow with the student. Perhaps start with a simple text editor and "Run" button on the device for younger students, then gradually introduce a more sophisticated IDE on PC for older students (with features like debugging, project management). The underlying compiler changes, but the experience evolves smoothly. Pitfall to avoid: A sudden jump in tooling complexity at age 16. If a student who used only a simple editor is suddenly thrown into a professional IDE with dozens of panels, they'll be lost. Provide an intermediate step or a streamlined interface for the cross-compiler suited to high school novices.  
- 5. Develop Comprehensive Teacher Training: The success of this strategy hinges on teachers. Offer training workshops and create teacher guides that not only cover SuperPascal language specifics but also pedagogy (e.g., how to explain memory using the Zeal Computer, how to debug student code systematically). Emphasize the why behind the two-tier model so teachers buy in to the philosophy. Pitfall to avoid: Neglecting teacher comfort – if teachers find the system too daunting or don't understand its benefits, they may circumvent it or convey negativity to students. Also avoid assuming all teachers have prior Pascal experience; train from basics up.  
- 6. Utilize Offline Nature for Focus: Turn the lack of internet into a pedagogical strength. Incorporate more hands-on exercises that involve the hardware (e.g., using the Zeal's LEDs, buttons, sensors if available) so students see computing's real-world side, something online coding often misses. Pitfall to avoid: Do not isolate computing from fun or relevance – just because it's offline doesn't mean it should feel antiquated. Include creative projects (games, simple graphics, music generation) to keep motivation high without internet  $^{40}$ $^{41}$ .  
- 7. Foster Progression in Hardware Understanding: Start with simple analogies for CPU and memory (Phase 1), move to actually showing assembly or memory dumps (Phase 2), and by Phase 3 let students play with real low-level tools (e.g., an assembler, or stepping through code at the instruction level). This scaffolding aligns with cognitive readiness  $^{28}$ $^{29}$ . By 12th grade, they should

conceptually know how high-level code translates to machine code on the Z80 - a powerful insight before heading to university. Pitfall to avoid: Jumping straight to complex architecture or, conversely, never moving beyond vague metaphors. Students might get bored if kept at an overly basic level too long; increase challenge as they mature.

- 8. Integrate Evaluation and Feedback Mechanisms: Implement periodic evaluations of student outcomes under this new system versus previous methods. Track metrics like problem-solving ability, code quality, and engagement. Collect feedback from teachers on curriculum pacing and from students on what excites or frustrates them. Use this data to fine-tune the approach. For example, if many 16-year-olds struggle with the jump to OOP, perhaps introduce a simplified OOP concept at 15 as a bridge. Pitfall to avoid: A static curriculum – don't assume the initial plan will be perfect. Remain agile and ready to adjust instructional materials, examples, or even language features (with backward compatibility) based on classroom reality.  
- 9. Provide Rich Library Support and Documentation: To prevent frustration, ensure both compilers come with well-documented libraries for common tasks (graphics, sound, math, text handling). Young students won't write everything from scratch - having a DrawSprite or PlayTone routine available can enable projects that inspire them. Document these libraries with examples. Pitfall to avoid: Overloading the subset with too many library calls that obscure fundamental learning. Libraries should support and motivate (e.g., let them make a simple game), but core assignments should still reinforce algorithmic thinking (not just calling black-box functions).  
- 10. Plan for Transition to Other Languages: While SuperPascal can be the primary vehicle through high school, provide a capstone module where students see that their skills transfer to, say, Python, C++, or Java (whatever is needed next). For instance, demonstrate a simple algorithm in Pascal then in another language, highlighting similarities. This reassures students (and parents and universities) that no doors are being closed by focusing on Pascal. Indeed, history shows Pascal-trained students easily pick up other languages  $74 \quad 70$ . Pitfall to avoid: Insularity – do not give the impression that "Pascal is the only way". It's a means to an educational end, not a cult. Emphasize concepts over syntax so they see that they've learned computing, not just one language.  
- 11. Encourage an Ecosystem and Community: Beyond formal classes, encourage the formation of coding clubs, hackathons or competitions using the Zeal Computer and SuperPascal (perhaps a national student competition for best Pascal game or project). Community excitement can sustain interest (akin to how the BBC Micro had user clubs and magazines in the 80s  $75$ $76$ ). Open up the platform for tinkering – maybe some students will extend it (writing their own assembly routines or even contributing to the compiler as a learning exercise). Pitfall to avoid: Over-control – don't lock down the device so much that curious students can't explore beyond the curriculum. For example, if a 17-year-old wants to write a small OS for the Zeal or add a new feature to the language, that initiative should be encouraged, not stymied (provided it's done in safe contexts). An open ethos will keep the pipeline of innovation flowing.  
- 12. Long-Term Maintenance Strategy: Formally establish a committee or working group (including educators, ministry reps, and technical experts) to oversee the SuperPascal ecosystem. This group ensures updates (for compatibility, for new OS/hardware, etc.) are done in a coordinated manner, and that the curriculum remains up-to-date. Also plan for hardware maintenance – maybe provide spare parts or a repair guide for the Zeal Computer to schools. Pitfall to avoid: Letting the system

fall into neglect after initial rollout. Many educational initiatives have failed because after 5 years, no one updated software or replaced aging equipment. Budget for continuous support must be earmarked. We recommend a modest annual budget for refreshing materials and doing a hardware audit in schools.

By following these recommendations, the two-tier compiler strategy will not only avoid potential pitfalls but actively turn them into strengths. We will create a robust educational pipeline where each stage is optimized for the students' cognitive level, and each transition is smooth by design. This ensures that by the end, we have well-rounded young developers who understand computing from the transistor-switching level all the way to high-level software design.

# Forward-Looking Roadmap for Implementation and Adoption

To successfully implement the offline-first SuperPascal ecosystem and achieve systemic adoption, we propose the following phased roadmap over the next several years. This roadmap is crafted to address technical roll-out, curriculum integration, teacher preparedness, and policy endorsement in parallel:

# Phase 0: Pilot and Development (Year 0 – Year 1)

- Prototyping (Months 1-6): Finalize the Zeal Computer hardware design and manufacture a small batch of units (e.g., 50-100) for testing. Complete the initial version of both compilers. At this stage, verify that the Z80 micro-compiler can compile a representative subset of programs within memory and speed constraints (for instance, ensure it handles a ~100-line student program in under a few seconds compile time). Simultaneously, get the Rust-based cross-compiler to successfully generate Z80 code and run simple OOP programs on the hardware. This is also the time to develop the basic IDE/editor for the micro-compiler environment. Success metric: By month 6, have a working prototype where a sample lesson (like “Hello World” or a loop printing 1-10) can be written and executed on the Zeal by a student-tester.

- Pilot Curriculum Development (Months 3-9): Assemble a team of experienced CS educators to write pilot lesson plans and exercises for a couple of grades (perhaps 5th grade for the subset and 11th grade for the full compiler usage) as a proof of concept. Incorporate pedagogical research  $^{31}$  - for example, use scaffolding techniques in the lesson plan (start each unit with guided examples, move to independent projects). Design assessments to measure outcomes ( quizzes, practical tasks).

- Teacher Training - Pilot Cohort (Months 6-12): Select a small group of tech-savvy teachers (say 5-10 teachers) who are enthusiastic about CS education to be the first trainees. Conduct an intensive workshop where they themselves go through the "student" experience of learning on the Zeal with SuperPascal. Provide them draft curriculum and get their feedback. This will both refine teaching materials and create champion teachers who can later help train others.

- Policy Engagement (Months 6-12): Begin dialogue with the Ministry of Education and relevant curriculum boards. Present the plan, along with initial prototype demos and alignment with new policy (like the Bulgarian no-internet rule). Use data or research citations to bolster the case (for instance, show how integrated environments like Turbo Pascal improved learning speed in the past  $^{15}$ , or how offline learning can improve focus  $^{77}$ ). The goal is to secure an official pilot program approval – meaning the Ministry allows (or even funds) running this in a few schools as a trial. Also engage parent associations early to build support; emphasize the safety and focus aspects that address their concerns  $^{56}$ .

# Phase 1: Pilot Implementation (Year 1 - Year 2)

- School Pilot Rollout (Academic Year 1): Introduce the system in a small number of schools – perhaps 3-5 schools, covering different regions or school types (urban, rural, etc.) to gather diverse data. Likely target

one class per relevant grade in those schools. For instance, pick a grade 5 class and a grade 9 class (to trial both tier-1 and tier-2 usage). Provide each student in pilot classes either a personal Zeal unit or ensure ample devices in a lab setting. Implement the pilot curriculum. Technical support personnel should be on-call to handle any device issues or software bugs that appear in real classroom use.

- Monitoring and Evaluation (Concurrent with pilot): Collect data constantly. Have observers (or video, with consent) in some classes to see how students interact with the device and language. Administer pre- and post-pilot assessments to measure improvement in computational thinking skills. Survey both students and teachers about engagement and difficulties. For example, measure if students can solve certain algorithmic problems more reliably than a control group not using the system. Also track any technical difficulties (e.g., "compiler error messages were confusing" or "devices had keyboard latency issues") and log them for engineering fixes.

- Refinement (During school breaks or end of Year 1): Use the pilot feedback to refine both the technology and curriculum. Maybe the pilot shows that 5th graders struggled with the concept of data types – teachers report this, and the curriculum team then adds more analogies or exercises to reinforce that concept. Or perhaps the micro-compiler needs a new feature (like a simple debugging command) because teachers found students making certain mistakes frequently. Implement improvements in the off months. Also, refine teacher training materials based on pilot teacher feedback – maybe they needed more help on explaining assembly, so add a section in training for that.

- Evidence and Advocacy (End of Year 1): Prepare a pilot report with quantitative outcomes and qualitative testimonials. E.g., "In pilot School X,  $85\%$  of 7th graders could correctly write a loop to sum numbers vs  $60\%$  in the previous curriculum  $^{29}$ . Teacher Y says: 'Students are far more engaged and actually understand what the computer is doing under the hood." Use this report to advocate for scaling up. Ideally, by now the Ministry sees positive results and agrees to expand the program. If there were weaknesses identified, also outline how we will address them in the scaled rollout - showing responsiveness.

# Phase 2: Scaling Up (Year 2 - Year 5)

- Curriculum Integration and Expansion (Year 2): Finalize a full curriculum for all intended grade levels (e.g., if introducing from grade 5 through 12, ensure all those syllabi are ready or at least a few months ahead of classroom use). Align it with national standards in detail and get it officially approved as either a replacement or an alternative pathway for CS education. Develop textbooks or e-resources for students - likely interactive e-books that can be used offline on the Zeal or a companion tablet, if available.

- Teacher Training - Broad (Year 2-3): Roll out large-scale teacher professional development. Perhaps create an online training module to complement in-person workshops, so that training can reach dozens or hundreds of teachers. Use the pilot teachers as co-trainers or peer mentors - new teachers will value hearing from those who already taught with the system. Aim to have every teacher who will teach the following year's classes trained at least 3-6 months in advance. Also set up a support forum or chat group for teachers to share tips and ask questions during the year.

- Hardware Production and Distribution (Year 2-4): Once approved for scale, produce the Zeal Computer in larger quantities. We'd negotiate manufacturing - possibly locally if the aim is to also stimulate local tech economy. Ensure quality control (each device pre-tested). Develop a plan for device maintenance - maybe equip each school with a spare set and basic troubleshooting guide (the hardware is relatively simple, but teachers should know how to, say, reflash an FPGA if needed or swap a faulty cable). For distribution, coordinate with school schedules so devices arrive before the term starts. If budgets allow, consider giving students individual units they can use at home (this is excellent for practice, especially since they're safe offline). If not, ensure sufficient lab time in school.

- Phased Grade Introduction (Year 3-5): It might be wise not to introduce across all grades in one go, but roll up year by year. For example, start in Year 3 with grades 5, 6, and 9 (just an example set), then next year

add grade 7 and 10, etc. This way, teachers and students who started continue onward and we build capacity gradually. However, if urgency and resources permit, a faster rollout could be done - but then heavy support is needed to avoid any hicups causing backlash. Keep evaluating each year; e.g., after first full year of national rollout for some grades, do an evaluation similar to pilot to ensure consistency (maybe results are great in some schools but lagging in others - then find out why; perhaps some teachers need additional support).

- Community Building (Year 3+): Encourage formation of a SuperPascal users community. For instance, organize an annual "Zeal Computing Olympiad" or fair where students showcase projects made on the platform. Invite education officials to see these - it's persuasive to see a 14-year-old explaining how they wrote a game in assembly-spiced Pascal on an 8-bit computer! This builds excitement and keeps momentum. Possibly create a nationwide online repository (still accessible in a controlled way) for students to share code (maybe moderated by teachers - an offline system can still share code via teacher uploading to a central system).

- International Collaboration (Year 4-5): If the system is successful nationally, consider sharing it with or adopting it in neighboring regions or countries with similar education needs. This can attract external validation and possibly funding. It also increases the developer and user base for the platform, which aids sustainability. For example, if other Eastern European countries or developing regions with limited internet adopt it, they might contribute improvements or content. However, ensure this doesn't divert focus from local implementation – best to solidify at home first.

# Phase 3: Consolidation and Sustainability (Year 5 and beyond)

- Full Pipeline Graduation (Year 5-7): By this time, the first cohorts who started in middle school with SuperPascal are reaching graduation. Evaluate their outcomes: Are more of them choosing CS or engineering in university? How do their skills compare to peers taught under old curricula? Perhaps track a sample into university to see if they perform better in CS101 than others (the hypothesis is they will, given the depth of their prep). Use these successes to solidify support from the Ministry for the long haul (budget allocations, etc.). This is also the time to adjust any misalignments: e.g., if we find that universities still require knowledge of language X, ensure we cover X briefly or ensure students can transition (though likely unnecessary if foundation is strong).

- Refresh Technology (Year 5-10): Plan for iterative updates to hardware or software. For instance, maybe consider a Zeal Computer 2.0 with minor improvements gleaned from usage (could be ergonomic improvements, more memory if needed, etc.) while remaining backward compatible. Or if the FPGA allows, perhaps releasing a "mode" for more advanced use (like an enhanced graphics mode or a faster CPU mode) for students who want to push boundaries. Keep software up to date with host PC OS (e.g., ensure the cross-compiler and IDE run on the latest Windows/Mac/Linux as they evolve). By year 10, evaluate if the platform is still meeting needs or if a major overhaul is needed (for instance, maybe by then introducing a 16/32-bit successor for high school while keeping Z80 for middle school - pure speculation, but we should remain open to evolution). The key is to ensure continuity: any new generation device should run old student code, and any new software features should not break what teachers already know.

- Institutionalize and Archive Knowledge: Work with educational authorities to make the SuperPascal curriculum part of the standard teaching repertoire. Universities training new CS teachers should include a module on this ecosystem so incoming teachers are already familiar. Create archival documentation (perhaps a "SuperPascal Handbook") that can be used to train new personnel years down the line. Essentially, move from a startup-style project to a stable program integrated into the education system.

- Stay Innovative: Even as it becomes routine, continue to look for enhancements. For instance, perhaps integrate aspects of algorithm visualization (maybe the IDE could show a memory map changing as program runs - offline but very instructive). Or incorporate more interdisciplinary projects (like using the

Zeal for data logging in physics experiments). Keep the experience fresh and connected to real world to avoid stagnation or student fatigue.

This roadmap ensures that within about 5 years, the two-tier compiler strategy goes from concept to full deployment, with iterative improvements and evidence-based validation at each step. By pacing the rollout and rigorously supporting teachers and students, we maximize the chances of long-term success. After a decade, we anticipate a self-sustaining program producing technologically adept young adults and serving as a model for how to teach computing in a focused, offline-first manner. The result will be a generation that not only can use modern technology, but truly understands it - precisely the outcome desired by educators and industry alike.
